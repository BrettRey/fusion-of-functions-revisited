\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

When one expression seems to have two functions at once, what should persuade us that it does? In \mention{some apples}, \mention{some} determines the noun phrase, and the noun \mention{apples} is its head. In \mention{I'll take \bk{\fu{some}}}, \mention{some} is the whole noun phrase, and there's no noun to be its head. One influential analysis gives \mention{some} both functions there: it's the determiner of its noun phrase and the phrase's head at once. This paper asks what evidence could justify that doubling.

The doubling is called \term{fusion of functions}. Its most explicit statements are in \textit{The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language} (\textit{CGEL}; \citealp{huddleston2002}) and in Payne, Huddleston, and Pullum (\citeyear{Payne2007}; henceforth PHP). In \mention{take some}, \textit{CGEL} analyses \mention{some} as a fused determiner--head. In \mention{the lucky few} and \mention{the rich}, \mention{few} and \mention{rich} are fused modifier--heads, at once modifier and head (pp.~410--421). PHP make the device explicit, extend it to \mention{once}, \mention{twice}, and \mention{thrice}, and say what it costs.

It departs from canonical structure twice: a constituent has two functions, and a phrase can have a head of another category, as when a noun phrase is headed by a determinative or an adjective rather than a noun (PHP, pp.~570--571). The question is when a grammar should accept the first.

Many syntacticians would sidestep it. The determinative could head the whole noun phrase, as on the DP hypothesis \citep[see][]{millerpullum2022tete}, or a silent noun could follow \mention{some} \citep{lobeck1995ellipsis,kester1996adjectival}. Each route gives up one of three commitments that \textit{CGEL} argues for and this paper keeps, since they're the premises that led to fusion (Section~\ref{sec:analyses}). They don't decide between fusion and the alternative introduced below, so the choice falls to evidence of form. The result is conditional on those commitments, but the criterion can be applied to a second function in any analysis.

The alternative I call \term{ordinary Head}. The phrase that \textit{CGEL} fuses has one function, head of the \term{nominal}, as a common noun does in \mention{take \bk{apples}}. The nominal is the unit between noun and noun phrase, such as \mention{old man} in \mention{the old man}. Since the two analyses describe the same strings, choosing between them takes a criterion, and I put the burden on the second function. A property of form counts as evidence for it if it varies with that function and not with the word alone, and the evidence is weighed against the extra structure (Section~\ref{sec:analyses}).

The test finds no such property where a word that's elsewhere a modifier heads a nominal, as in \mention{the lucky few}, \mention{the rich}, and \mention{nylon} in \mention{I prefer cotton shirts to \bk{\fu{nylon}}} (Sections~\ref{sec:analyses} and~\ref{sec:mod-head}), and \mention{once}, \mention{twice}, and \mention{thrice} add nothing further (Section~\ref{sec:once}).

For a determinative standing alone, the test finds one, the absence of an adjective before it (\ungram{\mention{Lucky few escaped}}).\footnote{As in \textit{CGEL}, * marks an unacceptable form, \judgmark{?} a questionable one, \judgmark{!} a non-standard one, and \judgmark{\%} one found only in some varieties.} Fusion derives that from adjacency, where ordinary Head states a condition of a kind \textit{CGEL} already states for pronouns (Sections~\ref{sec:det-head} and~\ref{sec:mod-head}). Section~\ref{sec:costs} weighs the advantage against the fewer functions ordinary Head assigns and the constituents with two parents it avoids, and I take the balance to favour ordinary Head, more clearly for the modifier function than for the determiner function, though not to force it.

Fused relatives are another matter (Section~\ref{sec:fused-relatives}). In \mention{\bk{\fu{Whoever} told you that} can't have read the report}, \mention{whoever} is head of the subject noun phrase and also subject of the relative clause (\textit{CGEL}, p.~1076). On the alternative, the head analysis, the relative word simply heads the nominal, with the clause as a modifier in it \citep{bresnan1978free}. But the second function shows in form, twice categorically and once as a preference. Fused relatives relativize their subject, which bare relatives (those with no relative word) can't do in standard English. They're never infinitival, though bare relatives can be. And in usage the form of \mention{whoever} answers to the function inside the clause as well as to the function of the whole phrase. Fusion gets all three from the rules for relative clauses with a relative phrase, where the head analysis needs statements that apply to no other head. So a second function is favoured there, though not forced, which \textit{CGEL}'s grammar states as fusion, and fused relatives are the one construction where this paper keeps it.
