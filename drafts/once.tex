\section{\mentionhead{Once}, \mentionhead{twice}, and \mentionhead{thrice}}\label{sec:once}

\textcite{Payne2007} built their case for fusion on \mention{once}, \mention{twice}, and \mention{thrice}. They distinguish five uses of \mention{once}: numerical (\mention{I will only say this to you once}), singulative (\mention{Not once have I seen anything like this}), past (\mention{I was once a teenage werewolf}), term of office (\mention{the once mayor of New York}), and sequential, which they analyse as a preposition like \mention{after}, with no fusion (pp.~584, 592, 594). \mention{Twice} and \mention{thrice} have the numerical and term-of-office uses, and a multiplier use (pp.~594--596).

Much of their argument concerns category. Against an adverb analysis, numerical \mention{once} takes determiners and relative clauses, freely and not only in idioms (\mention{four times for every once that the large pulley goes round}, p.~586 and n.~28), and it can function as subject (\mention{Once is enough}, p.~587). Against the noun analysis of some dictionaries (p.~587), it takes the pre-head modifiers of \mention{one}: \mention{at least once} beside \mention{at least one time}, \mention{more than once} beside \mention{more than one time} (p.~589). It's a determinative, and on ordinary Head it heads the nominal of its NP, so what's in question is again only the second function.

They take the form of the word to show that function too. In \mention{someone} and the other compound determinatives \enquote{the morphological compounding in this case reflects the fusion of two syntactic functions}, and \mention{once} is \enquote{similarly} a compound, of a determinative base \mention{on·} and a noun base \mention{·ce} \enquote*{time} (pp.~587--588). But simple \mention{some} and \mention{few} are fused with no compounding, and Payne et al.'s own note takes \mention{·ce} to be a reanalysed genitive ending (p.~588 n.~30). What the two bases mirror is the word's meaning, a quantifier with a sort, as in the compounds (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}), and both analyses give \mention{once} that meaning.

The first thing fusion is said to explain is why \mention{once} occurs both with and without a determiner, which a singular count noun doesn't. Numerical \mention{one} functions as determiner (\mention{one time}) and as modifier after a determiner (\mention{this one time}), so \mention{once} is a fused determiner-head on its own and a fused modifier-head after \mention{this} (p.~588). That's the link between the two uses that Section~\ref{sec:mod-head} examined for \mention{few}, a default with exceptions both ways. Ordinary Head states it as a permission: \mention{once} may head a nominal with or without a determiner.

The second is the adjective. Since the fused \mention{once} is also the determiner, \ungram{\mention{last once}} is excluded \enquote{for the same reason as} \ungram{\mention{last one time}}, while \mention{this last once} is fine (p.~589). One of their \textit{JL} referees judged \mention{this last once} ungrammatical, and they allow that speakers may vary (p.~586 n.~26). COCA, NOW, and iWeb have neither \mention{this last once} nor \mention{the last once}, but they have the pattern with \mention{twice}, their (33b) (p.~595), as in \mention{\bk{The last twice} I checked my 71~GT I got 29 to the gallon} (mgexp.com).\footnote{Searched by hand on 18 October 2026. With \texttt{this last.[md*] once}, \mention{last} tagged as an ordinal, COCA and NOW have no hits, and iWeb's four lines have sequential \mention{once} (\mention{How long does this last once the oils are mixed?}) or a slip for \mention{this last one}. With \mention{the last} before \mention{once}, \mention{twice}, or \mention{thrice}, no line has \mention{once} heading a nominal: most are slips for \mention{one} or \mention{ounce}, or have \mention{once} as an adverb or the sequential preposition. NOW has 290 tokens of \mention{the last twice}, and all 180 of its displayed lines have \mention{twice} heading the NP, 176 of them from British or Irish sources, most of them racing reports (\mention{He's gone well \bk{the last twice}}, \textit{Racing Post}, 2026). iWeb has 28 such lines of 39, and COCA none.} So the adjective after a determiner is attested, though not with \mention{once}. The gap before a bare \mention{once} is the one Section~\ref{sec:mod-head} weighed for \mention{few}.\footnote{In 97 COCA lines of \mention{a}, a word that can be tagged as an adjective, and \mention{once} or \mention{twice}, none has an adjective before \mention{once} or \mention{twice} heading a nominal after \mention{a}, as in \mention{a lucky few}. The adjective comes before \mention{once} only in the noun \mention{once-over} (\mention{a quick once-over}, 17 lines) and in \mention{once-in-a-lifetime} (3). In the rest, \mention{once} or \mention{twice} is outside the NP (\mention{I saw \bk{a psychic} once}). Nothing here calls for the statement that \mention{a lucky few} needs on both analyses.} Fusion derives it from adjacency, and ordinary Head excludes it by its condition on pre-head adjectives, a further statement of a kind the grammar already contains. Their further example, \ungram{\mention{\pn{this} once last}} with a silent head after \mention{last} (p.~589), separates fusion from a silent head, not from ordinary Head.

Larger phrases go the same way. A DP such as \mention{at least once} or \mention{more than once}, or a PP such as \mention{up to twice}, functions for Payne et al.\ as fused determiner-head (pp.~589--590 and n.~31), and after a determiner as fused modifier-head (\mention{not the more than once which would have been permitted}, pp.~590--591). Ordinary Head puts each in the head function alone. A fused determiner-head is also head of the nominal, so the PP \mention{up to twice} heads a nominal on both analyses. In \mention{once more}, \mention{more} is a post-head modifier on both, as in \mention{something more} (p.~591).

Singulative and past \mention{once} take no determiner and no attributive adjective, though they take relative clauses (\mention{Not once that I can think of}, \mention{Once when the opera Aida was about to be performed}) (p.~592). Payne et al.\ trace both gaps to singulative \mention{one}, which \enquote{always functions as a determiner, not as a modifier} (p.~592). For the determiner, that's the default of Section~\ref{sec:mod-head} again, which both analyses follow here. For the adjective, fusion derives the gap from adjacency and ordinary Head from its condition, the trade already counted for \mention{few}.

Term-of-office \mention{once} functions as attributive modifier (\mention{the once mayor}), as does \mention{twice} in \mention{the twice president}, which many speakers find unnatural (pp.~593, 595). Both analyses have to state that the use is largely confined to names of offices (\ungram{\mention{my once job as a glass-blower}}, pp.~585, 593). For Payne et al., \mention{once} here \enquote{will act as a fused modifier-head forming a nominal rather than forming a full NP} (p.~593), so the attribute is a nominal whose head is also its modifier. On ordinary Head the determinative \mention{once} simply functions as modifier in the nominal \mention{once mayor}, as \mention{two} does in \mention{two books}. That's one function where fusion has two, and one nominal fewer (Figure~\ref{fig:once}), though the lexical statement is no shorter.

The rest of their case doesn't bear on the second function. Multiplier \mention{twice} (\mention{twice the amount}) functions as predeterminer modifier (p.~596), and in \mention{once a week} \mention{once} is head (p.~596 n.~40), on both analyses. Their non-standard uses (pp.~596--597) turn on category and on \mention{once} heading a nominal, which the analyses share: in \nonstd{\mention{at once or another}}, \mention{or another} reaches the base \mention{on·}, as in \mention{somebody or another}, and \nonstd{\mention{the once and only time}} coordinates the nominal \mention{once} with \mention{only time}. In \nonstd{\mention{the twice the amount}}, ordinary Head has \mention{twice} heading the nominal where Payne et al.\ have a fused modifier-head (p.~597), as in \mention{just the twice} (p.~595).

No property of form in this paradigm varies with the second function rather than with the word,\footnote{The genitive, which divides the fused determiner-heads word by word (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}), is absent from all the uses: COCA, NOW, and iWeb have no genitive of \mention{once}, \mention{twice}, or \mention{thrice}. Ordinary Head adds the three words to its list of gaps. Fusion predicts the gap, unless it lets a compound's nominal base carry the genitive, as in \mention{everyone's} (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}), and then \mention{·ce} needs an exception.} so on the criterion of Section~\ref{sec:analyses} the paradigm fusion was developed on is neutral between the analyses. Where their structures differ, in the term-of-office use, ordinary Head has the fewer functions.
