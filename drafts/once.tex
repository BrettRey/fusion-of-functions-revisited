\section{\mentionhead{Once}, \mentionhead{twice}, and \mentionhead{thrice}}\label{sec:once}

PHP built their case for fusion on \mention{once}, \mention{twice}, and \mention{thrice}. They distinguish five uses of \mention{once}: numerical (\mention{I will only say this once}), singulative (\mention{Not once have I seen anything like this}), past (\mention{I was once a teenage werewolf}), term of office (\mention{the once mayor}), and sequential (\mention{once he arrived}), which they analyse as a preposition like \mention{after}, with no fusion (pp.~584, 592, 594). \mention{Twice} and \mention{thrice} have the numerical and term-of-office uses, and a multiplier use (pp.~594--596).

Much of their argument concerns category. Against an adverb analysis, numerical \mention{once} takes determiners and relative clauses, freely and not only in idioms (\mention{for every once that the large pulley goes round}, p.~586 and n.~28), and it can function as subject (\mention{Once is enough}, p.~587). Against the noun analysis of some dictionaries (p.~587), it takes the pre-head modifiers of \mention{one}: \mention{at least once} beside \mention{at least one time}, \mention{more than once} beside \mention{more than one time} (p.~589). It's a determinative, and on ordinary Head it heads the nominal of its NP, so what's in question is again only the second function.

They take the form of the word to show that function too. In \mention{someone} and the other compound determinatives \enquote{the morphological compounding in this case reflects the fusion of two syntactic functions}, and \mention{once} is \enquote{similarly} a compound, of a determinative base \mention{on·} and a noun base \mention{·ce} \enquote*{time} (pp.~587--588). But simple \mention{some} and \mention{few} are fused with no compounding, and PHP's own note takes \mention{·ce} to be a reanalysed genitive ending (p.~588 n.~30), and \textcite[89]{wu2021exocentric} finds their noun base \enquote{far from convincing}. The two bases can as well reflect the word's meaning, a quantifier with a sort, as in the compounds (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}), and both analyses give \mention{once} that meaning.

The first thing fusion is said to explain is why \mention{once} occurs both with and without a determiner, which a singular count noun doesn't. Numerical \mention{one} functions as determiner (\mention{one time}) and as modifier after a determiner (\mention{this one time}), so \mention{once} is a fused determiner--head on its own and a fused modifier--head after \mention{this} (p.~588). That's the link between the two uses that Section~\ref{sec:mod-head} examined for \mention{few}, a default with exceptions both ways. Ordinary Head states it as a permission: \mention{once} may head a nominal with or without a determiner.

The second is the adjective. Since the fused \mention{once} is also the determiner, \ungram{\mention{last once}} is excluded \enquote{for the same reason as} \ungram{\mention{last one time}}, while \mention{this last once} is fine (p.~589). One of their \textit{JL} referees judged \mention{this last once} ungrammatical, and they allow that speakers may vary (p.~586 n.~26). The searches of COCA, NOW, and iWeb found no relevant example of \mention{this last once} or \mention{the last once}, but they found the pattern with \mention{twice}, their (33b) (p.~595), as in \mention{\bk{The last \fu{twice}} I checked my 71~GT \ldots} (mgexp.com).\footnote{Searched with \mention{last} tagged as an ordinal and as a plain string. No line in the three corpora clearly has \mention{once} heading a nominal after \mention{this last} or \mention{the last}; the hits are the verb \mention{last}, slips for \mention{one} or \mention{ounce}, and adverbial or sequential \mention{once}. NOW has 290 tokens of \mention{the last twice}, all 180 displayed lines with \mention{twice} heading the NP, 176 of them British or Irish, mostly racing reports (\mention{He's gone well \bk{the last \fu{twice}}}, \textit{Racing Post}); iWeb has 28 such lines of 39, COCA none.} So the adjective after a determiner is attested, though not with \mention{once}. The gap before a bare \mention{once} is the one Section~\ref{sec:mod-head} weighed for \mention{few}.\footnote{In 97 COCA lines of \mention{a}, a word that can be tagged as an adjective, and \mention{once} or \mention{twice}, none has an adjective before \mention{once} or \mention{twice} heading a nominal after \mention{a}, as in \mention{a lucky few}. The adjective comes before \mention{once} only in the noun \mention{once-over} (\mention{a quick once-over}, 17 lines) and in \mention{once-in-a-lifetime} (3). In the rest, \mention{once} or \mention{twice} is outside the NP (\mention{I saw \bk{a psychic} once}). These lines give nothing that calls for the statement \mention{a lucky few} needs on both analyses.} Fusion derives it from adjacency, and ordinary Head excludes it by its condition on pre-head adjectives, a further statement of a kind the grammar already contains. Their further example, \ungram{\mention{\pn{this} once last}} with a silent head after \mention{last} (p.~589), separates fusion from a silent head, not from ordinary Head.

Larger phrases and the other uses go the same way. A DP such as \mention{more than once}, or a PP such as \mention{up to twice}, is for PHP a fused determiner--head on its own and a fused modifier--head after a determiner (pp.~589--591 and n.~31), and ordinary Head puts each in the head function alone. Singulative and past \mention{once} take no determiner and no attributive adjective (p.~592), and both gaps fall under what's already been weighed: the missing determiner as a lexical restriction stated on both analyses, the adjective under the trade counted for \mention{few}.

Term-of-office \mention{once} functions as attributive modifier (\mention{the once mayor}), as does \mention{twice} in \mention{the twice president}, which many speakers find unnatural (pp.~593, 595). Both analyses have to state that the use is largely confined to nouns for offices (\ungram{\mention{my once job as a glass-blower}}, pp.~585, 593). For PHP, \mention{once} here \enquote{will act as a fused modifier-head forming a nominal rather than forming a full NP} (p.~593), so the attribute is a nominal whose head is also its modifier. On ordinary Head the determinative \mention{once} simply functions as modifier in the nominal \mention{once mayor}, as \mention{two} does in \mention{the two books}. That's one function where fusion has two, and one nominal fewer (Figure~\ref{fig:once}), though the lexical statement is no shorter.

The rest of their case (multiplier \mention{twice}, \mention{once a week}, and the non-standard uses, pp.~595--597) turns on category and on the word heading a nominal, which the analyses share.

Apart from the adjective restriction weighed in Section~\ref{sec:mod-head}, no property of form in this paradigm varies with the second function rather than with the word,\footnote{The genitive, which divides the fused determiner--heads word by word (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}), is absent from all the uses: the searches of COCA, NOW, and iWeb found no genitive of \mention{once}, \mention{twice}, or \mention{thrice}. Ordinary Head adds the three words to its list of gaps. For the uses fusion treats as Det--Head, \textit{CGEL}'s case argument predicts the gap, unless fusion lets a compound's nominal base carry the genitive, as in \mention{everyone's} (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}), and then fusion would have to set \mention{·ce} apart from bases such as \mention{-one}.} so on the criterion of Section~\ref{sec:analyses} the paradigm fusion was developed on adds no evidence of its own. Where their structures differ, in the term-of-office use, ordinary Head has the fewer functions.
