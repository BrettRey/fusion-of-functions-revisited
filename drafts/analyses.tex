\section{Two analyses and a criterion}\label{sec:analyses}

\textit{CGEL}'s framework keeps categories apart from functions. A category is a set of words or phrases, such as noun, determinative, or adjective. A function is the relation a constituent bears within its phrase, such as head, determiner, or modifier. The two vary independently: \mention{nylon} is a noun functioning as head in \mention{made of \bk{nylon}} and as modifier in \mention{a \bk{nylon} shirt} (\textit{CGEL}, p.~421), and an NP functions as determiner in \mention{\bk{Kim's} book}.

So \term{determinative} names a category (\mention{the}, \mention{this}, \mention{some}, \mention{every}, \mention{many}), and \term{determiner} names a function. Figure~\ref{fig:analyses}a gives \textit{CGEL}'s structure for \mention{some apples}. The determinative heads a determinative phrase (DP) that functions as determiner (Det) of the NP.\footnote{DP here is \textit{CGEL}'s determinative phrase, a phrase whose head is a determinative, not the DP of the DP hypothesis, on which the determinative heads the whole noun phrase. PHP (p.~566 n.~2), like \textit{CGEL}, take the noun as the NP's ultimate head.} The noun heads the nominal (Nom), and the nominal is the NP's head. Where nothing turns on the difference, I say that a word has a function when the phrase it heads has it (\mention{some} is determiner in \mention{some apples}).

The NP's \term{ultimate head} is the word reached by following head relations down from the NP: \mention{apples} in Figure~\ref{fig:analyses}a, \mention{some} in b and~c.

Without a noun, as in \mention{I'll take \bk{\fu{some}}}, the nominal still needs a head. \textit{CGEL}'s answer is fusion of functions: the DP functions as determiner and as head at once, a fused determiner--head (Det--Head; Figure~\ref{fig:analyses}b) (pp.~410--421). After a determiner, as in \mention{the lucky few}, the word is a fused modifier--head (Mod--Head). PHP make the device explicit and list where English uses it: \enquote{The subtypes of dependent involved are determiner, modifier and, in fused relative clauses, subject or prenucleus} (p.~571; the prenucleus is the function of a fronted relative phrase). Sections~\ref{sec:det-head}, \ref{sec:mod-head} and~\ref{sec:fused-relatives} take them in that order, and Section~\ref{sec:once} takes PHP's own case, \mention{once}, \mention{twice}, and \mention{thrice}.

Fusion, as PHP define it, departs from canonical structure in two ways. First, one constituent has two functions, so that it \enquote{will in effect have two mothers}, which they call \enquote{the major departure from canonical principles of structure} (p.~570). The structure is then a directed acyclic graph rather than a tree \citep{pullumRogers2008Expressive}. In Det--Head, the shared DP has two parents, the NP and the nominal; in Mod--Head, both functions usually belong to one nominal (pp.~582, 588), though in their tree for \mention{the more than once which would have been permitted}, the fused DP has a parent in each of two nominals (p.~590). Second, an NP can have an ultimate head of another category: the fused node is \enquote{a point in the normal path of headedness at which there can be a change of category} (p.~569).

Ordinary Head keeps the second departure and drops the first. Whatever phrase \textit{CGEL} fuses functions as head of the nominal and nothing else: a DP for \mention{some} (Figure~\ref{fig:analyses}c) and for \mention{few} in \mention{the lucky few}, an AdjP in \mention{the rich}. For adjectives this is close to the traditional analysis, though there the adjective heads the noun phrase itself: \enquote{Adjectives can function as heads of noun phrases} \citep[421]{quirk1985}. A determiner before it, as \mention{the} in \mention{the rich}, is the NP's determiner as usual, and the nominal keeps its other dependents: in \mention{few of them}, the \mention{of}-phrase is a complement in the nominal headed by \mention{few}, as in \textit{CGEL}'s own partitive tree (pp.~411--412). What fusion states as a licence for a fused function, ordinary Head states as a \term{permission}: a word may head a nominal, with or without a determiner before it. \mention{Some} may head one with no determiner, and \mention{rich} one after \mention{the}.

\textit{CGEL} calls a head with no second function a \term{pure head} (p.~410), and it considers the analysis itself, as \enquote{change of function without change of category}, with \mention{many} a determiner in \mention{\bk{Many people} would agree with you} and a head in \mention{\bk{\fu{Many}} would agree with you} (p.~421).

The change of category needs no new kind of structure. PHP say it \enquote{will be independently required in the case of hybrid constructions} (p.~571), and their tree for \mention{there's \bk{no stopping him}} has a VP as head of an NP whose determiner is \mention{no}: \enquote{an unexpected category appears as head \ldots\ However, there is no fusion of functions in this case} (p.~600). The tree shares the two properties that matter here, an unexpected category as head and no constituent with two functions, though the VP heads the NP directly, with no nominal between. PHP find hybrids \enquote{only marginally in English} (p.~566), which might seem to count against ordinary Head. But ordinary Head changes category only where fusion does, and at the same node, so it uses no departure that fusion doesn't also use, and uses it no more widely.

Both analyses keep three commitments that \textit{CGEL} and PHP argue for, and the comparison holds them fixed. First, in \mention{some apples} the ultimate head is \mention{apples}, not \mention{some} (Figure~\ref{fig:analyses}a). Second, the determinative keeps its category when it stands alone: it takes the modifiers it takes before a noun (\mention{not much cheese}, \mention{not much of the cheese}), which \enquote{argues against any part-of-speech distinction between the two uses} (\textit{CGEL}, p.~420). Third, there's no silent head noun. Analyses that give up one of these avoid fusion by other means: a determiner phrase headed by the determinative, or a silent noun.\footnote{A silent noun is recovered from an antecedent on ellipsis analyses \citep{lobeck1995ellipsis} and needs none on empty-noun analyses \citep{kester1996adjectival,saab2019nominal,kayne2007several,aarts2024fused}. \textcite{wu2021exocentric} puts an empty noun after \mention{the rich} and after a determinative standing alone (pp.~19--20, 118, 173, 184--185). \textit{CGEL} gives its grounds against ellipsis on pp.~420--421, and PHP note a problem for a null head (p.~580 n.~17).}

This paper doesn't take those analyses on. Its result is conditional: given \textit{CGEL}'s commitments, the balance favours a second function only in fused relatives. Holding \textit{CGEL}'s categories fixed isolates the question of functions; it doesn't endorse them. Indeed, \textcite{reynolds2026determinativesnouns} argues that determinatives are a kind of noun, beside common nouns, proper nouns, and pronouns. On that classification, a determinative heading a nominal is a noun heading it, so the second departure disappears for determinatives on both analyses, leaving ordinary Head with no departure for them and fusion with the first.

Since both analyses describe the same strings, the comparison needs a criterion. Interpretation separates analyses that predict different readings, as it separates both of these from a silent \mention{people} (Section~\ref{sec:mod-head}). But fusion and ordinary Head predict the same readings, from the same sources.

\textit{CGEL} classifies fused heads twice, by function (p.~410) and by interpretation (pp.~333, 411). In its simple type, a head recoverable from an antecedent could be added (\mention{Four boys played croquet and \bk{\fu{two}} played tennis}, \enquote*{two boys}), in the partitive type, an \mention{of} phrase, stated or understood, gives the set (\mention{He made ten mistakes but \bk{\fu{most}} were fairly trivial}), and in the special type, the reading is fixed for the word (\mention{the rich}, \enquote*{rich people}). The classifications cross: \textit{CGEL}'s examples put Det--Head in all three types and Mod--Head in the partitive and the special.

\textit{CGEL} also gives these readings to a head with no second function. \mention{Other} heading a nominal has a plural, \mention{others}, so \textit{CGEL} takes it to be a noun, with the structure \enquote{simply determiner + head} and \enquote{no syntactic fusion of two functions} (pp.~1517--1518): \mention{the others}, \enquote*{other boxes}; \mention{the other}, \enquote*{the other one of the plates}; \mention{others}, \enquote*{other people}.

So the reading comes from an antecedent or the situation, from a partitive, or from a convention that both analyses list word by word (\textit{CGEL} lists \mention{few}, \mention{many}, \mention{all}, and \mention{some} as taking \enquote*{people}, p.~414). The second function supplies none of these. Each analysis still needs a route to the reading. What's left is form, which is what \textit{CGEL} rests the determiner function on when it argues from the genitive (p.~420). PHP call a constituent with two functions a departure from canonical structure, so I take the burden to lie with the second function.

Where the same category and head relation account for an expression, a property of form counts as evidence for a second function if it varies with that function and not with the word alone. By form, I mean what can be observed of an expression: the shapes of its words (a case form such as \mention{whom}, or a variant such as \mention{none}, which occurs only without a noun after it), their order, and which dependents and determiners occur with each. Interpretation alone isn't form.\footnote{\textit{CGEL} reasons this way in declining to call a content clause after a verb an object: unlike an NP, it needn't follow the verb directly (\mention{He denied categorically that \ldots}, \ungram{\mention{He opened slowly the door}}; p.~1018). A bare determinative patterns with the NP (\ungram{\mention{He took slowly some}}), so the criterion doesn't favour less structure as such.}

The criterion says what counts as evidence for a second function, not how much is enough. Whether such evidence outweighs the extra structure is a separate question, and a fact that something else could also explain weighs less. So the later sections compare the analyses construction by construction: what each predicts, where the data go against a prediction, what each has to state instead, and what the extra structure buys. Where the data can't separate the analyses, I say so, since finding no evidence for a second function isn't finding evidence against it. \textit{CGEL} itself names a form that would count: a fused determiner--head should resist the genitive, because \enquote{for full acceptability its case should match both functions} (p.~420).

Examples are \textit{CGEL}'s or PHP's where cited, attested where a source is given, and otherwise constructed. Judgments not attributed to a source are mine, and claims about what's excluded are for standard English. Corpus lines come from COCA, NOW \citep{davies2016now}, and iWeb \citep{davies2018iweb}, searched in September and October 2026; where a display was capped, counts are of the lines shown. Some sets of lines were classified by a large language model (Claude Opus~5.5), and each line's label is kept so that the classification can be checked. The genitive lines in Section~\ref{sec:det-head} were labelled in two runs of the model, the second blind, which found the same genitives. I checked the labels for the verb-object searches in Section~\ref{sec:fused-relatives} and 105 from the other searches behind Sections~\ref{sec:det-head} and~\ref{sec:fused-relatives}, and changed five, none affecting a figure the paper reports. The record behind each corpus claim, with its query, date, counts, and labels (but not the corpus lines), is listed in an \href{https://github.com/BrettRey/fusion-of-functions-revisited/blob/master/EVIDENCE.md}{evidence index}.

The criterion answers \textit{CGEL}'s objection to ordinary Head. \mention{Nylon} in \mention{I prefer cotton shirts to \bk{\fu{nylon}}} is one of \textit{CGEL}'s fused modifier--heads, of the type denoting composition (p.~417). \textit{CGEL} rejects analysing it as a noun functioning simply as head, since that \enquote{would not distinguish this construction from \mention{I prefer cotton to nylon}}, where \mention{nylon} isn't \enquote{understood as having modifier function} (p.~421). The difference is real. But the word is the same in both sentences, and so is its form: bare \mention{nylon} as the object of \mention{to}. What differs is the antecedent. In the first sentence, \mention{cotton shirts} supplies the kind of item, and \mention{nylon} supplies the material, as it does in \mention{This shirt is nylon}. Fusion needs the antecedent too: the modifier function supplies no shirts.

The one formal property \textit{CGEL} ties to these modifier--heads is their determiners. The NPs of \mention{nylon}'s type \enquote{are either bare or have a definite determiner}, with \enquote{significant loss of acceptability when the determiner is indefinite} (p.~417), and nouns of other types are excluded, as in \ungram{\mention{Bill likes the linguistics lecturer, but I prefer \bk{the \fu{sociology}}}} (p.~415). The examples in (\ref{ex:dog}) cross both lines.

\ea\label{ex:dog}
\ea \mention{You take the cat shirt and I'll take \bk{the \fu{dog}}.} (\enquote*{the shirt with a dog on it})
\ex \ungram{\mention{You take the cat shirt and I'll take \bk{\fu{dog}}.}}
\ex \mention{You take the cotton shirt and I'll take \bk{\fu{nylon}}.}
\ex \mention{a misdemeanor sentence rather than \bk{a \fu{felony}}} (\textit{Arizona Daily Star}, 2019, COCA)
\z\z
\mention{Dog} and \mention{felony} belong to no type \textit{CGEL} lists, and they work, which leaves \ungram{\mention{the sociology}} unexplained on either analysis.

Bare \mention{dog} is out and \mention{a felony} is fine, as for a singular count noun generally. Non-count \mention{nylon} stands bare for a single item and resists \mention{a} (\marg{\mention{I bought a cotton shirt rather than \bk{a \fu{nylon}}}}, p.~417). So the determiners follow the countability of \mention{dog}, \mention{felony}, and \mention{nylon}, which would be modifiers in the full NPs (\mention{the dog shirt}, \mention{a felony sentence}, \mention{a nylon shirt}), not that of \mention{shirt} or \mention{sentence}, the understood noun, which is count throughout. Both analyses make the overt noun the head, so both predict that. A silent head doesn't, without a further provision: one that took the understood noun's features would reject bare \mention{nylon}, and one with no features of its own wouldn't exclude bare \mention{dog}. So nothing in the determiners varies with the modifier function, and the restriction \textit{CGEL} states for it doesn't hold.\footnote{Dependents give the same result. \textcite[28--29]{matthews2014} notes that material nouns used as modifiers take \mention{partly} and \mention{largely} (\mention{a partly cotton shirt}) and suggests they're adjectives there. But the nouns take them with no modifier function too (\mention{I prefer cotton to \bk{partly nylon}}; \mention{This lava is \bk{partly magma}}, conserve-energy-future.com), so \mention{partly} isn't tied to the modifier function.} On this criterion, nothing here counts as evidence for the modifier function.\footnote{\textit{CGEL}'s other example, \mention{Mary earns \bk{\fu{double}}} (p.~421), fuses head and predeterminer modifier (p.~418), and \mention{double}, a noun for \textit{CGEL} (p.~421), gets the treatment \mention{nylon} gets. Its category fits. Before a definite determiner, \textit{CGEL}'s predeterminer modifiers include \mention{all} and \mention{both}, nouns and NPs (\mention{half the cake}, \mention{double the number of applications}), and \mention{twice} (pp.~433--434), and my searches of COCA \citep{davies2008cocaonline} found no adjective phrase there, apart from one marginal \mention{400-fold the amount} (a blog).}
