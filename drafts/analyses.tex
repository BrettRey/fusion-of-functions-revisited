\section{Two analyses and a criterion}\label{sec:analyses}

The framework keeps categories apart from functions. A category is a set of words or phrases, such as noun, determinative, or adjective. A function is the relation a constituent bears within its phrase, such as head, determiner, or modifier. The two vary independently: \mention{nylon} is a noun functioning as head in \mention{made of \bk{nylon}} and as modifier in \mention{a \bk{nylon} shirt} \citep[421]{huddleston2002}, and an NP functions as determiner in \mention{\bk{Kim's} book}.

So \term{determinative} names a category (\mention{the}, \mention{this}, \mention{some}, \mention{every}, \mention{many}), and \term{determiner} names a function.\footnote{One more function is used from Section~\ref{sec:det-head} on. A \term{peripheral modifier} modifies a whole NP, determiner included, as \mention{even} does in \mention{\bk{even} this house} \citep[331]{huddleston2002}.} Figure~\ref{fig:analyses}a gives \textit{CGEL}'s structure for \mention{some apples}. The determinative heads a determinative phrase (DP) that functions as determiner (Det) of the NP.\footnote{DP here is \textit{CGEL}'s determinative phrase, a phrase whose head is a determinative, not the DP of the DP hypothesis, on which the determinative heads the whole noun phrase. \textcite{millerpullum2022tete} draw the same distinction. \textcite[566 n.~2]{Payne2007}, like \textit{CGEL}, take the noun as head of the NP.} The noun heads the \term{nominal} (Nom), the unit between noun and NP, and the nominal is the NP's head. \textit{CGEL} recognizes the unit because \mention{old man} in \mention{the old man} is neither a noun nor an NP, since it can't function as subject or object (p.~329). I write \enquote{nominal} in prose and Nom in trees and quotations. The NP's \term{ultimate head} is the word reached by following head relations down from the NP: \mention{apples} in Figure~\ref{fig:analyses}a, \mention{some} in b and~c.

Without a noun, as in \mention{I'll take \bk{some}}, the nominal still needs a head. \textit{CGEL}'s answer is \term{fusion of functions}: the DP functions as determiner and as head at once, a fused determiner-head (Det--Head; Figure~\ref{fig:analyses}b) \citep[410--421]{huddleston2002}. After a determiner, as in \mention{the lucky few}, the word is a fused modifier-head (Mod--Head). \textcite{Payne2007} make the device explicit and list where English uses it: \enquote{The subtypes of dependent involved are determiner, modifier and, in fused relative clauses, subject or prenucleus} (p.~571). Sections~\ref{sec:det-head}, \ref{sec:mod-head} and~\ref{sec:fused-relatives} take them in that order, and Section~\ref{sec:once} takes Payne et al.'s own case, \mention{once}, \mention{twice}, and \mention{thrice}.

Fusion, as Payne et al.\ define it, departs from canonical structure in two ways. First, one constituent has two functions, so that it \enquote{will in effect have two mothers}, which they call \enquote{the major departure from canonical principles of structure} (p.~570). The structure is then a directed acyclic graph rather than a tree \citep{pullumRogers2008Expressive}, and the CGELBank treebank gives such a constituent a hyphenated label for its two parents \citep[§3.2]{reynolds2025cgelbankmanual}. In Det--Head the shared DP has two parents, the NP and the nominal; in Mod--Head both functions belong to one nominal (pp.~582, 588). Second, an NP can have an ultimate head of another category: the fused node is \enquote{a point in the normal path of headedness at which there can be a change of category} (p.~569).

\term{Ordinary Head} keeps the second departure and drops the first. Whatever phrase \textit{CGEL} fuses functions as head of the nominal and nothing else: a DP for \mention{some} (Figure~\ref{fig:analyses}c) and for \mention{few} in \mention{the lucky few}, an AdjP in \mention{the rich}. For adjectives this is the traditional analysis: \enquote{Adjectives can function as heads of noun phrases} \citep[421]{quirk1985}. A determiner before it, as \mention{the} in \mention{the rich}, is the NP's determiner as usual, and the nominal keeps its other dependents: in \mention{few of them} the \mention{of}-phrase is a complement in the nominal headed by \mention{few}, as in \textit{CGEL}'s own partitive tree (pp.~411--412). What fusion states as a licence for a fused function, ordinary Head states as a \term{permission}: a word may head a nominal, with or without a determiner before it. \mention{Some} may head one with no determiner, and \mention{rich} one after \mention{the}.

\textit{CGEL} has a term for a head of this kind: the independent genitive pronouns \enquote{occur in fused heads and also pure heads} (p.~410), a pure head being one with no second function. It also considers the analysis, as \enquote{change of function without change of category}, with \mention{many} a determiner in \mention{\bk{Many} people would agree with you} and a head in \mention{\bk{Many} would agree with you} (p.~421).

The change of category needs no new kind of structure. Payne et al.\ say it \enquote{will be independently required in the case of hybrid constructions} (p.~571), and their tree for \mention{there's \bk{no stopping him}} has a VP as head of an NP whose determiner is \mention{no}: \enquote{an unexpected category appears as head \ldots\ However, there is no fusion of functions in this case} (p.~600). The tree shares the two properties that matter here, an unexpected category as head and no constituent with two functions, though the VP heads the NP directly, with no nominal between. Payne et al.\ find hybrids \enquote{only marginally in English} (p.~566). But ordinary Head changes category in exactly the constructions where fusion does, at the same node, so it uses no departure that fusion doesn't also use, and uses it no more widely.

Both analyses keep three commitments that \textit{CGEL} argues for, and the comparison holds them fixed. First, where an NP has a noun, the noun is its head, and the determinative is a dependent, as in \mention{some apples}. Second, the determinative keeps its category when it stands alone: it takes the dependents it takes before a noun (\mention{not much cheese}, \mention{not much of the cheese}), which \enquote{argues against any part-of-speech distinction between the two uses} \citep[420]{huddleston2002}. Third, there's no silent head noun. Analyses that give up one of these avoid fusion by other means: a determiner phrase headed by the determinative, or a silent noun.\footnote{On the DP hypothesis the determinative heads the phrase \citep[see][]{millerpullum2022tete,salzmann2020npvsdp}, and pronouns can be analysed as determiners \citep{postal1966}; Payne et al.\ believe their fusion analysis of \mention{once} \enquote{not compatible with the \enquote*{DP hypothesis}, or at least that the hypothesis renders it difficult to find an appropriate analysis} (p.~566 n.~2). Silent nouns come in two kinds. On ellipsis analyses a noun is recovered from an antecedent \citep{lobeck1995ellipsis}. On empty-noun analyses a null noun with general content, such as \textsc{human}, needs none \citep{kester1996adjectival,panagiotidis2003empty,saab2019nominal}. \textcite{kayne2007several} gives \mention{few} a silent \textsc{number}, and \textcite{aarts2024fused} proposes empty heads in place of fusion. \textcite{wu2021exocentric} argues that no single analysis covers noun phrases without a head noun, and puts an empty noun after \mention{the rich} and after a determinative standing alone. He considers adjective and determinative heads and rejects them, since a head should share its phrase's category and a bare adjective can't show number or genericity (pp.~19--20, 118, 173). Ordinary Head makes the adjective head of the nominal, not of the NP; its change of category is Payne et al.'s second departure, which fusion shares; and \mention{the rich} is plural with no plural ending on every analysis, as Wu grants for his empty noun (p.~118 n.~14). \textit{CGEL}'s grounds against a silent head are that partitives have no noun to restore (\ungram{\mention{many ones of us}}, \ungram{\mention{much quantity of it}}); that \mention{the rich} is generic and \mention{the rich people} isn't; and that the NP construction lacks the marks of gapping, since it isn't confined to coordination and can't omit a sequence that isn't a constituent (pp.~420--421). \textcite[571 n.~5]{Payne2007} add that a null head needs stipulations that adjacency makes unnecessary.} This paper doesn't take those analyses on. Its result is conditional: given \textit{CGEL}'s commitments, fusion is needed only in fused relatives.

Since both analyses describe the same strings, the comparison needs a criterion. What they disagree about is structure, so the evidence that can decide between them is form, and \textit{CGEL} itself takes form as the sign of a function when it argues from the genitive (p.~420). Payne et al.\ call a constituent with two functions a departure from canonical structure, so I take the burden to lie with the second function. Where the same category and head relation account for an expression, a property of form counts as evidence for a second function if it varies with that function and not with the word alone. By form I mean the shape of words (a case form, or a form like \mention{none} that, in an NP, occurs only standing alone), order, and which dependents and determiners a word takes. Interpretation alone isn't form. \textit{CGEL} keeps the two apart elsewhere. In \mention{She considered Ed \bk{quite competent}}, the adjective phrase is predicated of \mention{Ed}, which is the object of \mention{considered}, and \textit{CGEL} calls \mention{Ed} the adjective phrase's \term{predicand}, a semantic relation, not its subject, a syntactic one (pp.~217, 252).

The criterion answers \textit{CGEL}'s one objection to ordinary Head. \mention{Nylon} in \mention{I prefer cotton shirts to \bk{nylon}} is one of \textit{CGEL}'s fused modifier-heads, of the type denoting composition (p.~417). \textit{CGEL} rejects analysing it as a noun functioning simply as head, since that \enquote{would not distinguish this construction from \mention{I prefer cotton to nylon}}, where \mention{nylon} isn't \enquote{understood as having modifier function} (p.~421). The difference is real. But the word is the same in both sentences, and so is its form: bare \mention{nylon} as the object of \mention{to}. What differs is what the context supplies.

The one formal property \textit{CGEL} ties to these modifier-heads is their determiners. The NPs of \mention{nylon}'s type \enquote{are either bare or have a definite determiner}, with \enquote{significant loss of acceptability when the determiner is indefinite} (p.~417), and nouns of other types are excluded, as in \ungram{\mention{Bill likes the linguistics lecturer, but I prefer \bk{the sociology}}} (p.~415). The examples in (\ref{ex:dog}) cross both lines.

\ea\label{ex:dog}
\ea \mention{You take the cat shirt and I'll take \bk{the dog}.}
\ex \ungram{\mention{You take the cat shirt and I'll take \bk{dog}.}}
\ex \mention{I bought a cat shirt rather than \bk{a dog}.}
\ex \mention{You take the cotton shirt and I'll take \bk{nylon}.}
\ex \mention{Choose a metal-bladed version rather than \bk{nylon}, as the latter will probably snap} (growveg.com, iWeb)
\ex \mention{\ldots\ to look more like a kitchen sink rather than \bk{a bathroom}} (thefarmersnest.com, 2012, COCA)
\ex \mention{punishing people convicted of drug possession with a misdemeanor sentence rather than \bk{a felony}} (\textit{Arizona Daily Star}, 2019, COCA)
\z\z

\mention{Dog}, \mention{bathroom}, and \mention{felony} belong to no type \textit{CGEL} lists, and they work. Bare \mention{dog} is out and \mention{a dog} is fine, as for a singular count noun generally. Non-count \mention{nylon} stands bare for a single item and resists \mention{a} (\marg{\mention{I bought a cotton shirt rather than \bk{a nylon}}}, p.~417). So the determiners follow the noun's own countability. Both analyses make the noun the head, so both predict that. A silent head doesn't: every antecedent in (\ref{ex:dog}) is a count noun, so a head that took its features would reject bare \mention{nylon}, and a head with none of its own wouldn't exclude bare \mention{dog}. What the facts don't show is anything that varies with the modifier function, and the restriction \textit{CGEL} states for it doesn't hold.\footnote{Dependents give the same result. \textcite[28--29]{matthews2014} notes that material nouns used as modifiers take degree words such as \mention{partly} and \mention{largely} (\mention{a partly cotton shirt}, \mention{largely glass walls}), and suggests they're adjectives there. The fused use takes them (\mention{I prefer pure cotton shirts to \bk{partly nylon}}), but so do uses with no modifier function: \mention{I prefer cotton to \bk{partly nylon}}, where the noun names the material, and \mention{This lava is \bk{partly magma}} (conserve-energy-future.com). So \mention{partly} isn't tied to the modifier function.} On this criterion, nothing here counts as evidence for the modifier function.\footnote{\textit{CGEL}'s other example, \mention{Mary earns \bk{double}} (p.~421), fuses a noun with a predeterminer modifier (p.~418), and \mention{double} gets the treatment \mention{nylon} gets. Its category fits. Before a definite determiner, \textit{CGEL}'s predeterminers are nouns and NPs (\mention{half the cake}, \mention{double the number of applications}) or the adverb \mention{twice} (p.~434), and my searches of the Corpus of Contemporary American English (COCA; \citealp{davies2008cocaonline}) found no adjective phrase there, apart from one marginal \mention{400-fold the amount} (a blog, 2012).}

\textit{CGEL} also names a form that a second function would show. A fused determiner-head should resist the genitive, because \enquote{for full acceptability its case should match both functions} (p.~420), and \textit{CGEL} points to case in fused relatives, where the form of \mention{whoever} or \mention{whomever} reflects its function inside the relative clause (pp.~1073--1074). That's a test the later sections can run. Among fused determiner-heads, \mention{either's} is fine and \mention{many's} all but absent, though \textit{CGEL} gives both words the same two functions (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}). After a preposition, \mention{whomever} is chosen far more often when it isn't the subject of the relative clause, though its function in the main clause stays the same (Section~\ref{sec:fused-relatives}). The first difference follows the word, the second the function.

The criterion says what counts as evidence for a second function, not how much is enough. A property of form can favour fusion without settling the matter, and an exception weakens the evidence without erasing it. So the later sections compare the analyses construction by construction: what each predicts, where the data go against a prediction, what each has to state instead, and what the extra structure buys. Where the data can't separate the analyses, I say so, since finding no evidence for a second function isn't finding evidence against it. The standard also applies to what this paper adds. Its analysis of \mention{a few} rests on number (Section~\ref{sec:mod-head}), and the licences ordinary Head needs for fused relatives count against it (Section~\ref{sec:fused-relatives}).
