\section{Modifiers as heads: Mod--Head}\label{sec:mod-head}

In Mod--Head fusion the head of a nominal is also its modifier. \textit{CGEL} uses it for words that function elsewhere as modifiers in a nominal: determinatives and adjectives, and also nouns such as \mention{nylon} (p.~417), taken up in Section~\ref{sec:analyses}:\footnote{Examples (\ref{ex:mh}a, b, d--f) are \textit{CGEL}'s (pp.~416--419); (\ref{ex:mh}c) is from NOW (\textit{National Observer}).}

\ea\label{ex:mh}
\ea \mention{This copy is defective but \bk{the other \fu{two}} are fine.}
\ex \mention{I didn't like it, but I respect the views of \bk{the \fu{many} who did}.}
\ex \mention{Are you one of \bk{the lucky \fu{few} who found powdered infant formula}?}
\ex \mention{\bk{The \fu{rich}} cannot enter the kingdom of Heaven.}
\ex \mention{How will it affect \bk{the \fu{very poor}}?}
\ex \mention{There were two pieces left and Kim chose \bk{the \fu{larger}}.}
\z\z

For \textit{CGEL}, \mention{two}, \mention{many}, \mention{few}, \mention{rich}, \mention{poor}, and \mention{larger} each modify the nominal and head it at once (pp.~415--419). PHP (pp.~579--580) draw the adjectival case for \mention{the very hard of hearing}: the AdjP is \enquote{head of Nom} and \enquote{modifier of that head}, and \mention{the} is the NP's determiner as usual.\footnote{PHP's tree labels the fused function Head--Mod, since the counterpart modifier follows the head (\mention{people very hard of hearing}); their tree for \mention{this once} has Mod--Head (p.~588). \textit{CGEL} allows fusion with a post-head modifier as well (p.~418). I write Mod--Head for both.} Unlike Det--Head, both functions belong to the same nominal (p.~588; Figure~\ref{fig:modhead}). Their main Mod--Head case, \mention{this once}, is taken up in Section~\ref{sec:once}. On ordinary Head the AdjP or DP is head of the nominal and has no other function.

So the two analyses agree that the adjective or determinative heads the nominal. On neither is a noun the NP's ultimate head; the category changes on the path of heads, where PHP accept it at the fused node (p.~569). Both keep the word's category. \mention{Rich} and \mention{poor} take adverbs (\mention{the extremely rich}, \mention{the very poor}), which \textit{CGEL} calls \enquote{clear evidence that they retain their category status as adjectives}, and \mention{the rich} is plural with no plural ending (p.~418). Both give the head the dependents it takes elsewhere: a partitive in \mention{the biggest of them} (p.~416), a post-head phrase in \mention{the pure in heart} (p.~418). \mention{Few} takes a partitive with a determiner and without one (\mention{few of them}, \mention{the few of us who stayed}), so its dependents don't distinguish its Det--Head use from its Mod--Head use. And neither has a silent noun, and adding one changes the reading: \mention{the people very hard of hearing} can't be read generically, as \mention{the very hard of hearing} is (PHP, p.~580).

\textit{CGEL} says the same of \mention{the rich people} (p.~420). \textcite{glass2019} adds readings that a silent \mention{people} would miss: ones not about people (\mention{The weak and mutated die, the healthy survive}, p.~10), and abstract ones, singular like \textit{CGEL}'s \mention{the immoral} (p.~418), as in \mention{The crazy \ldots{} is easily detected} (p.~7). She derives both from the adjective's own meaning (pp.~12--13), so on her account the adjective supplies all the description, with no noun for it to modify.\footnote{Glass places the operation in a silent nominalizing head, because the construction takes adjectives, \enquote{which generally only modify nominals} (p.~15). On ordinary Head the adjective phrase heads a nominal, which supplies the nominal without a silent head.} In \mention{the larger} (\ref{ex:mh}f), the context supplies the alternatives (the two pieces) and the comparative the property, and \mention{the} picks out the piece that has it.

What's at issue is only the second function, the modifier. Evidence for it would be a form found only in the use, as \mention{none} is for Det--Head (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}), a fact of order that only a modifier relation predicts, or determiners or a case that vary with the function. The formal differences PHP cite are in Det--Head and in fused relatives (\mention{none}, p.~581; the \mention{-ever} forms, p.~576), and they cite none for Mod--Head. Their fact of order is adjacency: with modifier and head one constituent, nothing can come between them, so \ungram{\mention{the in the room very hard of hearing}} is out (p.~580 n.~17). That argument is aimed at a silent head. On ordinary Head the PP would be a pre-head dependent in the nominal, which a PP never is (\ungram{\mention{the in the room people}}).

For determinatives, \textit{CGEL} links the fused use to the dependent one. Determinatives \enquote{which can function as internal modifier as well as determiner can in general occur as fused modifier-head as well as determiner-head} (\textit{CGEL}, p.~415): \mention{the few} beside \mention{the few mistakes}, where \mention{few} is modifier (p.~392). PHP (p.~588) state the link without the hedge: \mention{some}, \mention{every}, \mention{any}, and \mention{no} \enquote{can function solely as determiners and therefore never permit a preceding determiner}.

Yet \mention{some} is attested after \mention{the}, if marginally: \mention{\bk{the \fu{some}} will become the many}; \mention{one of \bk{the \fu{some} who hope for a return to the good old days}}.\footnote{COCA: \textit{Foreign Affairs}; iWeb: sspnet.org. COCA has five clear lines across searches for \texttt{the some}, \texttt{the some who}, and \texttt{the some of us who}; the other hits on the first display page of \texttt{the some} are typos for \mention{same}, the adverb \mention{some} \enquote*{approximately}, titles, and garbled text. iWeb has 10 hits for \texttt{the some who}, most with no earlier \mention{some} in view, so they aren't quotations of the word.} With \mention{the} as determiner, a fused \mention{some} can only be a modifier--head, and that's the Mod--Head PHP's \enquote{never} excludes. \textit{CGEL} hedges the licence (\enquote{in general}) and itself records words that head without modifying and words that modify without heading. \mention{Both}, which \enquote{cannot occur in internal modifier function}, heads a nominal after \mention{the} in \mention{the both of us} (p.~377). \mention{Every} and \mention{various} function as modifiers after a determiner (\mention{her every move}, \mention{the various options}) but don't head a nominal there: \enquote{\mention{Every} is again excluded, and so too this time is \mention{various}} (p.~416).

So the link between modifying after a determiner and heading a nominal after one is a default, not a condition. A determinative that functions as modifier after a determiner can usually head a nominal after one too, but the exceptions run both ways, and both analyses list the determinatives that can. Fusion does build one direction into the structure: a fused modifier--head is a modifier, so fusion predicts that a word with no modifier use can't head a nominal after a determiner. But \mention{the some} and \mention{the both of us} are exceptions in just that direction, and the words that modify without heading (\mention{every}, \mention{various}) have to be listed on both analyses. Ordinary Head states which determinatives can head a nominal after a determiner as permissions, as it does for those that stand alone (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}).

Which determiners NPs like \mention{the lucky few} take depends on the head word, not on a modifier function. With an adjective before \mention{few} and no noun after it, the determiner in the COCA lines searched is \mention{the}, a demonstrative (\mention{those chosen few}), a genitive (\mention{Hollywood's chosen few}), \mention{some}, \mention{we}, or \mention{us}: the determiners a plural noun takes. Nearly all the lines without one of these have \mention{a}, which here modifies \mention{few} inside its DP rather than determining the NP (below; Figure~\ref{fig:afew}).\footnote{COCA, searched by hand for \mention{lucky}, \mention{chosen}, \mention{fortunate}, \mention{remaining}, and \mention{priveledged} (misspelled in the search) before \mention{few}: of 321 lines with no following noun, 192 have \mention{the}, 90 \mention{a} alone, 25 a demonstrative, 6 a genitive, 2 \mention{some}, 2 \mention{we} or \mention{us}, one a name, and three nothing (a headline, a fragment, and a web post). Web, Indian, and Nigerian sources have more bare cases (\mention{work with \bk{select few} lenses}; \mention{the group of \bk{vicious \fu{few}} who \ldots}, legit.ng), with ordinary adjectives as well as \mention{select}, so what differs there is more likely the article than \mention{few}; on both analyses these NPs lack a determiner.}

Adjectives heading a nominal vary in the same way, word by word. The \mention{rich} type takes \mention{the} or a genitive (\mention{the world's poor}, \mention{our elderly}), though \textit{CGEL} says these NPs are \enquote{determined by the definite article} (p.~417).\footnote{In 500 COCA lines of \texttt{[apps*] poor|rich|elderly}, 19 have the adjective heading the NP after a genitive pronoun (\mention{care for \bk{their \fu{elderly}}}, \textit{New York Times}).} Some of its words take other determiners as well. \mention{Unemployed}, which \textit{CGEL} lists in the type, takes \mention{many} and \mention{some} in edited news (\mention{\bk{many \fu{unemployed}} say they are having trouble getting hired}, \textit{Washington Post}, 2021, NOW), while \mention{these rich} and \mention{some educated} turn up mostly in blogs and in news outside Britain and the United States.\footnote{In NOW, all 34 lines that \texttt{many/some unemployed [v*]} returned have \mention{unemployed} heading the nominal, after \mention{many} (18), \mention{some} (7), \mention{how many} (7), \mention{too many}, and \mention{so many}. The query \texttt{these rich [v*]} gave 14 such lines across COCA, iWeb, and NOW, and \mention{some educated} three utterances in NOW, against nine of \mention{educated guesses} and the like. \textcite[7]{glass2019} gives further web examples (\mention{Some fired say \ldots}, \mention{Many educated are far from role models}).} \mention{Blue} can head a nominal with no determiner (\mention{I like blue}), and \mention{second} one after \mention{a} (pp.~416--417). Both analyses state the range word by word.

What ordinary Head gives is the same function for \mention{few} in each of these uses: \mention{few would dare}, \mention{few of them}, \mention{the few}, and \mention{the lucky few}. In each, \mention{few} heads the nominal and has no other function; the determiner, if there is one, is another word, as \mention{the} is in \mention{the few}. Fusion gives the first two Det--Head and the last two Mod--Head, so the same word heading the same kind of NP gets a different pair of functions depending on whether a determiner precedes it.

Which adjectives can head a nominal is stated the same way on both analyses. \textit{CGEL} gives types, such as superlatives (\mention{the tallest}), ordinals (\mention{the second}), colours (\mention{I like \bk{\fu{blue}}}), and the special interpretation of \mention{the rich} (pp.~416--417), and excludes \ungram{\mention{the advancing}} (p.~415), \ungram{\mention{a bigger}}, and \ungram{\mention{the main}} (p.~416). \mention{Main} is attributive-only (p.~558), so modifier is its only function, and its exclusion is \textit{CGEL}'s evidence that \enquote{the possibility of fusion with the head must in the last resort be stated in syntactic terms} (p.~416). Ordinary Head states the same, as permissions to head a nominal.


The genitive gives no evidence for the modifier function. \textit{CGEL}'s argument from the genitive concerns the determiner function (p.~420), and it states no case requirement for modifiers, so the check here is this paper's own: whether the genitive divides the fused modifier--heads, some taking it and others not, as it divides the determiner--heads (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}). It does. \mention{The latter's} and \mention{the former's} are well attested, \mention{the poor's} occurs in edited prose (\mention{\bk{the \fu{poor}'s} material well-being}, \textit{Newsweek}), \mention{the rich's} only on the web, and \mention{few} after a determiner almost never takes it.\footnote{In one capped COCA display of \mention{the latter}, \mention{the former}, \mention{the other}, \mention{the poor}, and \mention{the rich} followed by \mention{'s}, \mention{the poor} has 8 genitives (five in magazines and academic writing) and \mention{the rich} 3 (all web or blog). \texttt{the few/many 's} gave none, and \texttt{few 's} one after a determiner (\mention{\bk{the first \fu{few}'s} salty freshness}, \textit{Ploughshares}). A cardinal after a determiner takes it freely: \mention{the two's} in 21 of 34 lines, ten in edited prose.} Here too, as with the determiner--heads, the functions these heads share don't predict which take the genitive.

Mod--Head is also where the advantage Section~\ref{sec:det-head} conceded to fusion has to be weighed. A bare determinative takes no pre-head adjective outside a few exclamations, taken up below (\ungram{\mention{Lucky few escaped}}),\footnote{\mention{Precious few would dare} isn't an exception. For \mention{precious} in \mention{They had precious little money}, \textcite[102]{matthews2014} offers the predicative use as the crucial evidence that it's an adverb (\mention{The money they had left was precious little}), and the same test works for \mention{few} (\mention{Volunteers were precious few}). So \mention{precious} is a modifier in the DP, as \mention{very} is in \mention{very few}, not in the nominal.} and fusion derives the gap from adjacency: with \mention{few} as Det--Head, \mention{lucky} would be in the nominal and yet before the determiner. After \mention{the}, \mention{few} is Mod--Head and \mention{lucky} modifies the nominal it heads. Ordinary Head states a condition: a nominal headed by a determinative takes a pre-head modifier only when the NP has a determiner (\mention{hardly} in \mention{hardly anyone} modifies the determinative, inside its DP).

\mention{A lucky few} looks like an exception to both fusion's derivation and ordinary Head's condition. Of the 321 COCA lines with an adjective before \mention{few} and no noun after it, 90 have \mention{a} as the only word before the adjective, and where a verb agrees with the phrase, it's plural: \mention{\bk{\fu{a lucky few}} were sent far and wide \ldots} (\textit{Washington Post}). The adjective can have its own modifier (\mention{A very lucky few}, CNN). \textit{CGEL} has two analyses that could apply. In one, \mention{a few} is a complex determinative whose internal structure is \enquote{sui generis}, admitting \mention{very}, \mention{good}, and \mention{fair}, and it's put among the lexical determinatives \enquote{as with the cardinal numerals} (p.~392). In the other, \mention{a} begins a sequence such as \mention{a further} or \mention{a good}, which allows a plural head only if a numeral or \mention{few} quantifies the head, as in \mention{\bk{A further few volunteers} were needed} (p.~353), and in \mention{a marvellous hundred days} \enquote{\mention{a} is determiner and quite independent of \mention{hundred}} (p.~385 n.~26).

Neither of \textit{CGEL}'s treatments of \mention{a few} favours fusion or ordinary Head. If \mention{a} is the NP's determiner, \mention{a lucky few} is built like \mention{the lucky few}: \mention{lucky} modifies the nominal after the determiner, so adjacency is undisturbed and ordinary Head's condition is met. If \mention{a} belongs to the determinative, so does \mention{lucky}: it modifies \mention{few} inside the DP, not the nominal. Either treatment needs a statement, a list of the sequences with \mention{a} that take a plural head or a slot after \mention{a} inside the determinative, and fusion and ordinary Head need the same one.

I take the analysis on which \mention{a} belongs to the determinative, in a form that gives \mention{a good few} one analysis where \textit{CGEL}'s two treatments would give it two (pp.~353, 392). In \mention{a hundred days}, \textit{CGEL} says that \mention{a}, which replaces \mention{one}, \enquote{must be considered as an integral part of the numeral, and not as an independent determiner}, since \enquote{the whole NP is clearly plural, not singular, as it would be if \mention{a} were the determiner} (p.~385). \textcite[7, 10]{reynolds2026numerals} argues that numerals above 99 are phrases, with the factor (\mention{one} in \mention{one thousand}) a modifier of the magnitude word, and that other modifiers can come between them (\mention{one whole thousand}).

On that analysis, extended to \mention{few}, \mention{a few} is a DP headed by \mention{few}, with \mention{a} as a modifier, and \mention{a very few}, \mention{a good few}, \mention{a further few}, and \mention{a lucky few} are the same DP with a modifier after \mention{a} (Figure~\ref{fig:afew}).\footnote{The slot is open. Besides \textit{CGEL}'s \mention{a good few} and \mention{a fair few} (p.~392), COCA has at least 28 adjectives in it before a plural noun, in at least 123 tokens (\mention{\bk{a mere few} feet}, \mention{\bk{a paltry few} pages}). \mention{A few} is positive where \mention{few} is negative (pp.~393--394), which I take to be a convention of the combination, as the minimizer reading of \mention{a bit} is in \mention{They didn't enjoy it \bk{a bit}} (p.~725).}

\mention{A little}, which \textit{CGEL} treats with \mention{a few} (p.~392), gets the same analysis. \mention{Quite} modifies the DP in \mention{quite a few} (p.~392), and the NP is plural, as \mention{a hundred days} is: on this analysis the plural comes from \mention{few} itself, where \textit{CGEL}'s treatment with \mention{a} as determiner needs the list of sequences (p.~353). So \mention{a lucky few} is no exception to the gap.

For PHP (p.~584), a condition like ordinary Head's is \enquote{precisely the kind of stipulation which is not required when the determiner and head functions are fused}. They say it of a silent-head analysis of the compounds, but it applies to ordinary Head's condition too. Whether the grammar needs such a statement anyway turns on the other heads with no determiner in their primary use, pronouns and proper nouns, and on three questions: whether such a head takes a pre-head dependent at all, whether it takes a non-restrictive adjective, and whether a restrictive one needs a determiner.

On the first, the grammar already makes a statement of this kind. Pronouns, which by definition \enquote{do not permit determiners} (p.~429), \enquote{do not normally allow internal pre-head dependents} (\ungram{\mention{Extravagant he bought a new car}}, p.~430), though they take some after the head (\mention{you who worked on both the projects}, p.~429). The compounds pattern the same way, with dependents after the head but not before, and for them \textit{CGEL} derives the gap from fusion (p.~423); a pronoun has no determiner function to derive it from. Ordinary Head's condition differs from the pronouns' restriction only in mentioning a determiner, which pronouns never take. So fusion's derivation saves one statement, of a kind the grammar already has.

On the second, proper nouns take a few non-restrictive adjectives with no determiner (\mention{poor Henry}; p.~520), and the core personal pronouns take \mention{lucky}, \mention{poor}, and \mention{silly} (\mention{Lucky you!}; p.~430). Simple determinatives don't seem to (\ungram{\mention{Lucky few!}}, \ungram{\mention{Poor many!}}), but two compounds are attested in the pronouns' exclamation, each just after the same exclamation with a proper noun or a pronoun: \mention{Poor Val. Poor everybody.} (\textit{New Yorker}), \mention{Lucky you. Lucky everyone.} (\textit{Everwood}).\footnote{COCA, \texttt{poor|lucky|silly everybody|everyone|somebody|someone}: these 2 of 28 lines; in the rest the adjective is predicative, comes before a clause (\mention{You're lucky someone called it in}), or follows a determiner, and no line has \mention{somebody} or \mention{someone} in the use.}

For \textit{CGEL} these are fused determiner--heads, for which a pre-head dependent is \enquote{not possible} (p.~423). Fusion could call them nouns by conversion here, like \mention{a nobody} (p.~423 n.~43), though they keep the compounds' meaning and take no determiner. It could list them as an exception to adjacency. Or it could take the adjective as a modifier of the whole NP, unlike \mention{lucky} in \mention{Lucky you!}, which \textit{CGEL} treats as a pre-head dependent (p.~430). On any of these, fusion needs an added statement for them. Ordinary Head adds them to \textit{CGEL}'s exception for \enquote{a few adjectives such as \mention{lucky}, \mention{poor}, \mention{silly} with the core personal pronouns}. So these exclamations need a further statement on both analyses and favour neither.

The third question is where pronouns and proper nouns look most like determinatives, but the likeness comes from countability. A restrictive adjective after a determiner is routine with personal pronouns (COCA has 350 tokens of \mention{the real me}), and the NP is singular (\mention{The real you is still alive \ldots}, \textit{Analog}).\footnote{In COCA, of the 14 lines for \texttt{the real you [be]}, ten have \mention{the real you} as subject, all with \mention{is} or \mention{'s}.} That is the pattern of a singular count head, which needs a determiner with or without a modifier. \textit{CGEL} would presumably call the word a common noun here, as it calls \mention{he} and \mention{she} in \mention{Is it a he or a she?} (p.~429 n.~46), though the reflexive suggests a secondary use of the pronoun (\mention{The 12-year-old me is kicking myself}). Proper nouns also take a restrictive adjective after a determiner: in \mention{The young Isaac Newton showed no signs of genius}, \mention{young} restricts a secondary use of the name in which determiners are \enquote{selected in the ordinary way} (pp.~520--521).

A plural head needs no determiner, and takes a restrictive adjective without one, a plural proper noun included (\mention{Lucky survivors were airlifted out}, \mention{Young Kennedys still go into politics}). So nothing that holds for proper nouns or for \mention{the real me} predicts \ungram{\mention{Remaining few were the hangers-on}} beside \mention{The remaining few were the hangers-on} (\textit{Christian Century}). Fusion's adjacency does.

So ordinary Head's condition is a further statement of a kind the grammar already contains, and the derivation of the pre-head gap, the one trace of the determiner function (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}), saves less than PHP suggest.

Nothing in the form of Mod--Head expressions, then, shows the modifier function. Which determinatives take an external determiner, which determiners they take, which adjectives head a nominal, and which heads take the genitive have to be stated word by word or type by type, on both analyses.
