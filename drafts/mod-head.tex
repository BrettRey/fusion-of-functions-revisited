\section{Modifiers as heads: Mod--Head}\label{sec:mod-head}

In Mod--Head fusion the head of a nominal is also its modifier. \textit{CGEL} uses it for words that function elsewhere as modifiers in a nominal: determinatives and adjectives, and also nouns such as \mention{nylon} (p.~417), taken up in Section~\ref{sec:analyses}:\footnote{Examples (\ref{ex:mh}a, b, d--f) are \textit{CGEL}'s \citep[416--419]{huddleston2002}; (\ref{ex:mh}c) is from NOW (\textit{National Observer}, 3 October 2022).}

\ea\label{ex:mh}
\ea \mention{This copy is defective but \bk{the other \fu{two}} are fine.}
\ex \mention{I didn't like it, but I respect the views of \bk{the \fu{many} who did}.}
\ex \mention{Are you one of \bk{the lucky \fu{few} who found powdered infant formula}?}
\ex \mention{\bk{The \fu{rich}} cannot enter the kingdom of Heaven.}
\ex \mention{How will it affect \bk{the \fu{very poor}}?}
\ex \mention{There were two pieces left and Kim chose \bk{the \fu{larger}}.}
\z\z

For \textit{CGEL}, \mention{two}, \mention{many}, \mention{few}, \mention{rich}, \mention{poor}, and \mention{larger} each modify the nominal and head it at once \citep[415--419]{huddleston2002}. \textcite[579--580]{Payne2007} draw the adjectival case for \mention{the very hard of hearing}: the AdjP is \enquote{head of Nom} and \enquote{modifier of that head}, and \mention{the} is the NP's determiner as usual.\footnote{Payne et al.'s tree labels the fused function Head--Mod, since the counterpart modifier follows the head (\mention{people very hard of hearing}); their tree for \mention{this once} has Mod--Head (p.~588). \textit{CGEL} allows fusion with a post-head modifier as well (p.~418). I write Mod--Head for both.} Unlike Det--Head, both functions belong to the same nominal (p.~588; Figure~\ref{fig:modhead}). Their main Mod--Head case, \mention{this once}, is taken up in Section~\ref{sec:once}. On ordinary Head the phrase is the head of the nominal and nothing more.

So the two analyses agree that the adjective or determinative heads the nominal. Neither has a noun as the NP's ultimate head, the change of category Payne et al.\ accept at the fused node (p.~569). Both keep the word's category. \mention{Rich} and \mention{poor} take adverbs (\mention{the extremely rich}, \mention{the very poor}), which \textit{CGEL} calls \enquote{clear evidence that they retain their category status as adjectives}, and \mention{the rich} is plural with no plural ending (p.~418). Both give the head the dependents it takes elsewhere: a partitive in \mention{the biggest of them} (p.~416), a post-head phrase in \mention{the pure in heart} (p.~418). \mention{Few} takes the same partitive with a determiner and without one (\mention{few of them}, \mention{the few of us who stayed}), so the dependents don't separate the Det--Head use from the Mod--Head one either. And neither restores a noun: \mention{the people very hard of hearing} can't be read generically, as \mention{the very hard of hearing} is \citep[580]{Payne2007}.

What's at issue is only the second function, the modifier. Form could show it in a form like \mention{none} (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}), in a fact of order that only a modifier relation predicts, or in the determiners and case the word takes. The formal differences Payne et al.\ cite are in Det--Head and in fused relatives (\mention{none}, p.~581; the \mention{-ever} forms, p.~576), and they cite none for Mod--Head. Their fact of order is adjacency: nothing comes between the fused functions, so \ungram{\mention{the in the room very hard of hearing}} is out (p.~580 n.~17). That argument is aimed at a silent head. With the AdjP as head, the PP would precede the head of a nominal, which PPs don't do before a noun either (\ungram{\mention{the in the room people}}).

For determinatives, \textit{CGEL} links the fused use to the dependent one. Determinatives \enquote{which can function as internal modifier as well as determiner can in general occur as fused modifier-head as well as determiner-head} \citep[415]{huddleston2002}: \mention{the few} beside \mention{the few mistakes}, where \mention{few} is modifier (p.~392). \textcite[588]{Payne2007} state the link without the hedge: \mention{some}, \mention{every}, \mention{any}, and \mention{no} \enquote{can function solely as determiners and therefore never permit a preceding determiner}.

Yet \mention{some} is attested after \mention{the}, if marginally: \mention{\bk{the \fu{some}} will become the many}; \mention{one of \bk{the \fu{some} who hope for a return to the good old days}}.\footnote{COCA: \textit{Foreign Affairs}, 1999; iWeb: sspnet.org. COCA has five clear lines across searches for \texttt{the some}, \texttt{the some who}, and \texttt{the some of us who}; the other hits on the first display page of \texttt{the some} are typos for \mention{same}, the adverb \mention{some} \enquote*{approximately}, titles, and garbled text. iWeb has 10 hits for \texttt{the some who}, most with no earlier \mention{some} in view, so they aren't quotations of the word.} With \mention{the} as determiner, a fused \mention{some} can only be a modifier--head, and that's the Mod--Head Payne et al.'s \enquote{never} excludes. \textit{CGEL}'s licence is hedged, and it records exceptions of both kinds itself. \mention{Both}, which \enquote{cannot occur in internal modifier function}, heads a nominal after \mention{the} in \mention{the both of us} (p.~377). \mention{Every} and \mention{various} function as modifiers after a determiner (\mention{her every move}, \mention{the various options}) but don't head a nominal there: \enquote{\mention{Every} is again excluded, and so too this time is \mention{various}} (p.~416).

So the link between the two uses is a default, not a condition. A determinative that functions as modifier after a determiner can usually head a nominal after one too, but the exceptions run both ways, and both analyses list the determinatives that can. Fusion does build one direction into the structure: a fused modifier--head is a modifier, so a word with no modifier use shouldn't head a nominal after a determiner. But \mention{the some} and \mention{the both of us} are exceptions in just that direction, and the other direction (\mention{every}, \mention{various}) is a list on both analyses. Ordinary Head states the list as a permission, as it states the list of determinatives that stand alone (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}).

The determiners these heads take don't show the modifier function. With an adjective before \mention{few} and no noun after it, COCA has \mention{the}, a demonstrative, a genitive, \mention{some}, \mention{we}, or \mention{us} (\mention{those chosen few}, \mention{Hollywood's chosen few}), all determiners of plural nouns; nearly all the rest have \mention{a}, which belongs to the DP (below; Figure~\ref{fig:afew}).\footnote{COCA, searched by hand for \mention{lucky}, \mention{chosen}, \mention{fortunate}, \mention{remaining}, and \mention{priveledged} (misspelled in the search) before \mention{few}: of 321 lines with no following noun, 192 have \mention{the}, 90 \mention{a} alone, 25 a demonstrative, 6 a genitive, 2 \mention{some}, 2 \mention{we} or \mention{us}, one a name, and three nothing (a headline, a fragment, and a web post). Web, Indian, and Nigerian sources have more bare cases (\mention{work with \bk{select few} lenses}; \mention{the group of \bk{vicious \fu{few}} who \ldots}, legit.ng, 2020), with ordinary adjectives as well as \mention{select}, so what differs there is more likely the article than \mention{few}; on both analyses these NPs lack a determiner.} The \mention{rich} type takes \mention{the} or a genitive (\mention{the world's poor}, \mention{our elderly}), though \textit{CGEL} says these NPs are \enquote{determined by the definite article} \citep[417]{huddleston2002}.\footnote{In 500 COCA lines of \texttt{[apps*] poor|rich|elderly} (18 October 2026), 19 have the adjective heading the NP after a genitive pronoun (\mention{care for \bk{their \fu{elderly}}}, \textit{New York Times}, 1992).} \mention{Blue} takes no determiner and \mention{second} takes \mention{a} (pp.~416--417). Both analyses state the range word by word, or type by type.

What ordinary Head gives is one set of functions for every use of \mention{few}: \mention{few would dare}, \mention{few of them}, \mention{the few}, and \mention{the lucky few}. In each, \mention{few} heads the nominal, and a determiner, when there's one, is the NP's own. Fusion gives the first two Det--Head and the last two Mod--Head, so the same word heading the same kind of NP gets a different pair of functions depending on whether a determiner precedes it.

Which adjectives can head a nominal is a list on both analyses. \textit{CGEL} gives types, such as superlatives (\mention{the tallest}), ordinals (\mention{the second}), colours (\mention{I like \bk{\fu{blue}}}), and the special interpretation of \mention{the rich} (pp.~416--417), and excludes \ungram{\mention{the advancing}} (p.~415), \ungram{\mention{a bigger}}, and \ungram{\mention{the main}} (p.~416). \mention{Main} is attributive-only (p.~558), so modifier is its only function, and its exclusion is \textit{CGEL}'s evidence that \enquote{the possibility of fusion with the head must in the last resort be stated in syntactic terms} (p.~416). Ordinary Head states the same list, as permissions to head a nominal.


Case shows no modifier function either. \textit{CGEL}'s argument from the genitive concerns the determiner function (p.~420), and it states no case requirement for modifiers, so the check here is this paper's own: whether the genitive divides the fused modifier--heads as it divides the determiner--heads (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}). It does. \mention{The latter's} and \mention{the former's} are well attested, \mention{the poor's} occurs in edited prose (\mention{\bk{the \fu{poor}'s} material well-being}, \textit{Newsweek}, 2010), \mention{the rich's} only on the web, and \mention{few} after a determiner almost never.\footnote{In one capped COCA display of \mention{the latter}, \mention{the former}, \mention{the other}, \mention{the poor}, and \mention{the rich} followed by \mention{'s}, \mention{the poor} has 8 genitives (five in magazines and academic writing) and \mention{the rich} 3 (all web or blog). \texttt{the few/many 's} gave none, and \texttt{few 's} one after a determiner (\mention{\bk{the first \fu{few}'s} salty freshness}, \textit{Ploughshares}, 1996). A cardinal after a determiner takes it freely: \mention{the two's} in 21 of 34 lines, ten in edited prose.} In these lines too, the shared functions don't predict the division.

Mod--Head is also where the advantage Section~\ref{sec:det-head} conceded to fusion has to be weighed. A bare determinative takes no pre-head adjective outside a few exclamations, taken up below (\ungram{\mention{Lucky few escaped}}),\footnote{\mention{Precious few would dare} isn't an exception. For \mention{precious} in \mention{They had precious little money}, \textcite[102]{matthews2014} offers the predicative use as the crucial evidence that it's an adverb (\mention{The money they had left was precious little}), and the same test works for \mention{few} (\mention{Volunteers were precious few}). So \mention{precious} is a modifier in the DP, as \mention{very} is in \mention{very few}, not in the nominal.} and fusion derives the gap from adjacency: with \mention{few} as Det--Head, \mention{lucky} would have to precede the determiner. After \mention{the}, \mention{few} is Mod--Head and \mention{lucky} modifies the nominal it heads. Ordinary Head states a condition: a nominal headed by a determinative takes a pre-head modifier only when the NP has a determiner (\mention{hardly} in \mention{hardly anyone} modifies the determinative, inside its DP).

\mention{A lucky few} looks like an exception for both. Of the 321 COCA lines with an adjective before \mention{few} and no noun after it, 90 have \mention{a} as the only word before the adjective, and where a verb agrees with the phrase, it's plural: \mention{\bk{\fu{a lucky few}} were sent far and wide \ldots} (\textit{Washington Post}, 2015). The adjective can be a phrase (\mention{A very lucky few}, CNN, 2005). \textit{CGEL} has two analyses that could apply. In one, \mention{a few} is a complex determinative whose internal structure is \enquote{sui generis}, admitting \mention{very}, \mention{good}, and \mention{fair}, and it's put among the lexical determinatives \enquote{as with the cardinal numerals} (p.~392). In the other, \mention{a} begins a sequence such as \mention{a further} or \mention{a good}, which takes a plural head if a numeral or \mention{few} quantifies it, as in \mention{\bk{A further few volunteers} were needed} (p.~353), and in \mention{a marvellous hundred days} \enquote{\mention{a} is determiner and quite independent of \mention{hundred}} (p.~385 n.~26).

Neither analysis separates fusion from ordinary Head. If \mention{a} is the determiner, \mention{few} follows a determiner, as in \mention{the lucky few}, and the adjective comes after it, so adjacency is undisturbed and ordinary Head's condition is met. If \mention{a} belongs to the determinative, so does the adjective, which then precedes \mention{few} inside the DP, not in the nominal. Either way the grammar needs a statement, a list of the sequences with \mention{a} that take a plural head or a slot inside the determinative, and the statement is the same on both analyses.

I take the analysis on which \mention{a} belongs to the determinative, in a form that gives \mention{a good few} one analysis where \textit{CGEL}'s two treatments would give it two (pp.~353, 392). In \mention{a hundred days}, \textit{CGEL} says that \mention{a}, which replaces \mention{one}, \enquote{must be considered as an integral part of the numeral, and not as an independent determiner}, since \enquote{the whole NP is clearly plural, not singular, as it would be if \mention{a} were the determiner} (p.~385). \textcite[7, 10]{reynolds2026numerals} argues that numerals above 99 are phrases, with the factor (\mention{one} in \mention{one thousand}) a modifier of the magnitude word, and that other modifiers can come between them (\mention{one whole thousand}). On that analysis, extended to \mention{few}, \mention{a few} is a DP headed by \mention{few}, with \mention{a} as a modifier, and \mention{a very few}, \mention{a good few}, \mention{a further few}, and \mention{a lucky few} are the same DP with a modifier after \mention{a} (Figure~\ref{fig:afew}).\footnote{The slot is open. Besides \textit{CGEL}'s \mention{a good few} and \mention{a fair few} (p.~392), COCA has at least 28 adjectives in it before a plural noun, in at least 123 tokens (\mention{\bk{a mere few} feet}, \mention{\bk{a paltry few} pages}). \mention{A few} is positive where \mention{few} is negative (pp.~393--394), which I take to be a convention of the combination, as the minimizer reading of \mention{a bit} is in \mention{They didn't enjoy it \bk{a bit}} (p.~725).}

\mention{A little}, which \textit{CGEL} treats with \mention{a few} (p.~392), gets the same analysis. Before a noun the DP is the determiner (\mention{a lucky few students}). On its own it heads the nominal, or for fusion is a Det--Head. \mention{Quite} modifies it in \mention{quite a few} (p.~392), and the NP is plural, as \mention{a hundred days} is: on this analysis the plural is the head's own, where \textit{CGEL}'s other route has to let these sequences with \mention{a} take a plural head (p.~353). That the adjective comes only after \mention{a}, as \mention{whole} comes after the factor in \mention{one whole thousand}, is stated on both analyses. So \mention{a lucky few} no longer counts against either. Fusion's derivation of \ungram{\mention{Lucky few escaped}} stands for \mention{lucky} as a modifier in the nominal, and the statement both analyses make excludes it inside the DP.

For \textcite[584]{Payne2007}, a condition of this kind is \enquote{precisely the kind of stipulation which is not required when the determiner and head functions are fused}. They say it of a silent-head analysis of the compounds, but it applies to ordinary Head's condition too. Whether the grammar needs such a statement anyway turns on the other heads with no determiner in their primary use, pronouns and names, and on three questions: whether such a head takes a pre-head dependent at all, whether it takes a non-restrictive adjective, and whether a restrictive one needs a determiner.

On the first, the grammar already makes a statement of this kind. Pronouns, which by definition \enquote{do not permit determiners} (p.~429), \enquote{do not normally allow internal pre-head dependents} (\ungram{\mention{Extravagant he bought a new car}}, p.~430), though they take some after the head (\mention{you who worked on both the projects}, p.~429). The compounds split the same way, and for them \textit{CGEL} derives the gap from fusion (p.~423); a pronoun has no determiner function to derive it from. Ordinary Head's condition has to mention a determiner, as the pronouns' needn't, so fusion's derivation saves a statement, but not a kind of statement the grammar would otherwise lack.

On the second, names take a few non-restrictive adjectives with no determiner (\mention{poor Henry}; pp.~520--521), and the core personal pronouns take \mention{lucky}, \mention{poor}, and \mention{silly} (\mention{Lucky you!}; p.~430). Simple determinatives don't seem to (\ungram{\mention{Lucky few!}}, \ungram{\mention{Poor many!}}), but two compounds are attested in the pronouns' exclamation, each just after the same exclamation with a name or a pronoun: \mention{Poor Val. Poor everybody.} (\textit{New Yorker}, 2005), \mention{Lucky you. Lucky everyone.} (\textit{Everwood}, 2004).\footnote{COCA, 18 October 2026, \texttt{poor|lucky|silly everybody|everyone|somebody|someone}: these 2 of 28 lines; in the rest the adjective is predicative, comes before a clause (\mention{You're lucky someone called it in}), or follows a determiner, and no line has \mention{somebody} or \mention{someone} in the use.} For \textit{CGEL} these are fused determiner--heads, for which a pre-head dependent is \enquote{not possible} (p.~423). Fusion can call them nouns by conversion here, like \mention{a nobody} (p.~423 n.~43), though they keep the compounds' meaning and take no determiner; list them as an exception; or take the adjective as a modifier of the whole NP, which separates them from \mention{Lucky you!} (p.~430). On any of these, adjacency's derivation needs qualifying. Ordinary Head adds them to \textit{CGEL}'s exception for \enquote{a few adjectives such as \mention{lucky}, \mention{poor}, \mention{silly} with the core personal pronouns}. So the exclamation needs further treatment on both analyses, and it doesn't separate them.

The third question is where pronouns and names look most like determinatives, but the likeness comes from countability. A restrictive adjective after a determiner is routine with personal pronouns (COCA has 350 tokens of \mention{the real me}), and the NP is singular (\mention{The real you is still alive \ldots}, \textit{Analog}, 2019).\footnote{COCA, 15 October 2026: of the 14 lines for \texttt{the real you [be]}, ten have \mention{the real you} as subject, all with \mention{is} or \mention{'s}.} \textit{CGEL} would presumably call the word a common noun here, as it calls \mention{he} and \mention{she} in \mention{Is it a he or a she?} (p.~429 n.~46), though the reflexive suggests a secondary use of the pronoun (\mention{The 12-year-old me is kicking myself}). Names are the same: in \mention{The young Isaac Newton showed no signs of genius}, \mention{young} restricts a secondary use in which determiners are \enquote{selected in the ordinary way} (pp.~520--521). A singular count head needs a determiner, with or without a modifier. A plural head needs none, and takes a restrictive adjective without one, a plural name included (\mention{Lucky survivors were airlifted out}, \mention{Young Kennedys still go into politics}). So nothing that holds for names or for \mention{the real me} predicts \ungram{\mention{Remaining few were the hangers-on}} beside \mention{The remaining few were the hangers-on} (\textit{Christian Century}, 1998). Fusion's adjacency does.

So ordinary Head's condition is a further statement of a kind the grammar already contains, the exclamation needs further treatment on both analyses, and the facts with a determiner follow from number and countability. The pre-head adjective remains the one trace of the determiner function (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}), but its derivation saves less than Payne et al.\ suggest.

Nothing in the form of Mod--Head expressions, then, shows the modifier function. Which determinatives take an external determiner, which determiners they take, which adjectives head a nominal, and which heads take the genitive have to be stated word by word or type by type, on both analyses.
