\section{Modifiers as heads: Mod--Head}\label{sec:mod-head}

In Mod--Head fusion the head of a nominal is also its modifier. \textit{CGEL} uses it for words that function elsewhere as modifiers in a nominal: determinatives and adjectives, and also nouns such as \mention{nylon} (p.~417), taken up in Section~\ref{sec:analyses}:\footnote{Examples (\ref{ex:mh}a, b, d--f) are \textit{CGEL}'s (pp.~416--419); (\ref{ex:mh}c) is from NOW (\textit{National Observer}).}

\ea\label{ex:mh}
\ea \mention{This copy is defective but \bk{the other \fu{two}} are fine.}
\ex \mention{I didn't like it, but I respect the views of \bk{the \fu{many} who did}.}
\ex \mention{Are you one of \bk{the lucky \fu{few} who found powdered infant formula}?}
\ex \mention{\bk{The \fu{rich}} cannot enter the kingdom of Heaven.}
\ex \mention{How will it affect \bk{the \fu{very poor}}?}
\ex \mention{There were two pieces left and Kim chose \bk{the \fu{larger}}.}
\z\z

For \textit{CGEL}, \mention{two}, \mention{many}, \mention{few}, \mention{rich}, \mention{poor}, and \mention{larger} each modify the nominal and head it at once (pp.~415--419). PHP (pp.~579--580) draw the adjectival case for \mention{the very hard of hearing}: the AdjP is \enquote{head of Nom} and \enquote{modifier of that head}, and \mention{the} is the NP's determiner as usual.\footnote{PHP's tree labels the fused function Head--Mod, since the counterpart modifier follows the head (\mention{people very hard of hearing}); their tree for \mention{this once} has Mod--Head (p.~588). \textit{CGEL} allows fusion with a post-head modifier as well (p.~418). I write Mod--Head for both.} Unlike Det--Head, both functions belong to the same nominal (p.~588; Figure~\ref{fig:modhead}). Their main Mod--Head case, \mention{this once}, is taken up in Section~\ref{sec:once}. On ordinary Head the AdjP or DP is head of the nominal and has no other function.

So the two analyses agree that the adjective or determinative heads the nominal, and both keep the word's category. \mention{Rich} and \mention{poor} take adverbs (\mention{the extremely rich}, \mention{the very poor}), which \textit{CGEL} calls \enquote{clear evidence that they retain their category status as adjectives}, and \mention{the rich} is plural with no plural ending on either analysis (p.~418). Both give the head the dependents it takes elsewhere: a partitive in \mention{the biggest of them} (p.~416), a post-head phrase in \mention{the pure in heart} (p.~418). \mention{Few} takes a partitive with a determiner and without one (\mention{few of them}, \mention{the few of us who stayed}), so its dependents don't distinguish its Det--Head use from its Mod--Head use. And neither has a silent noun, and adding one changes the reading: \mention{the people very hard of hearing} can't be read generically, as \mention{the very hard of hearing} is (PHP, p.~580).

\textit{CGEL} says the same of \mention{the rich people} (p.~420). \textcite{glass2019} adds readings that a silent \mention{people} would miss: ones not about people (\mention{The weak and mutated die, the healthy survive}, p.~10), and abstract ones, singular like \textit{CGEL}'s \mention{the immoral} (p.~418), as in \mention{The crazy \ldots{} is easily detected} (p.~7). She derives both from the adjective's own meaning (pp.~12--13).

What's at issue is only the second function, the modifier. Evidence for it would be a form that marks the modifier function, a fact of order that only a modifier relation predicts, or determiners or a case that vary with the function. The formal differences PHP cite are in Det--Head and in fused relatives (\mention{none}, p.~581; the \mention{-ever} forms, p.~576), and they cite none for Mod--Head. Their fact of order is adjacency: with modifier and head one constituent, nothing can come between them, so \ungram{\mention{the in the room very hard of hearing}} is out (p.~580 n.~17). That argument is aimed at a silent head. On ordinary Head the PP would be a pre-head dependent in the nominal, which a PP never is (\ungram{\mention{the in the room people}}).

For determinatives, \textit{CGEL} links the fused use to the dependent one. Determinatives \enquote{which can function as internal modifier as well as determiner can in general occur as fused modifier-head as well as determiner-head} (\textit{CGEL}, p.~415): \mention{the few} beside \mention{the few mistakes}, where \mention{few} is modifier (p.~392). PHP (p.~588) state the link without the hedge: \mention{some}, \mention{every}, \mention{any}, and \mention{no} \enquote{can function solely as determiners and therefore never permit a preceding determiner}.

Yet \mention{some} is attested after \mention{the}, if marginally: \mention{\bk{the \fu{some}} will become the many}; \mention{one of \bk{the \fu{some} who hope for a return to the good old days}}.\footnote{COCA: \textit{Foreign Affairs}; iWeb: sspnet.org. COCA has five clear lines among typos for \mention{same}, adverbial \mention{some}, titles, and garbled text, and iWeb has 10 hits for \texttt{the some who}, most with no earlier \mention{some} in view, so they aren't quotations of the word.} With \mention{the} as determiner, a fused \mention{some} can only be a modifier--head, and that's the Mod--Head PHP's \enquote{never} excludes. \textit{CGEL} hedges the licence (\enquote{in general}) and itself records words that head without modifying and words that modify without heading. \mention{Both}, which \enquote{cannot occur in internal modifier function}, heads a nominal after \mention{the} in \judgmark{\%}\mention{the both of us} (p.~377). \mention{Every} and \mention{various} function as modifiers after a determiner (\mention{her every move}, \mention{the various options}) but don't head a nominal there: \enquote{\mention{Every} is again excluded, and so too this time is \mention{various}} (p.~416).

So the link between modifying after a determiner and heading a nominal after one is a default, not a condition, and the exceptions run both ways. Fusion builds one direction in if it licenses a fused modifier--head through the word's modifier use, which is what keeps its licences general (Section~\ref{sec:costs}): a word with no modifier use then can't head a nominal after a determiner. But \textit{CGEL}'s own \mention{the both of us} is an exception in just that direction, and so, marginally, is \mention{the some}. Both analyses list the determinatives that can head a nominal after a determiner, ordinary Head as permissions, as for those that stand alone (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}).

Which determiners NPs like \mention{the lucky few} take depends on the head word, not on a modifier function. With an adjective before \mention{few} and no noun after it, the determiner in the COCA lines searched is \mention{the}, a demonstrative (\mention{those chosen few}), a genitive (\mention{Hollywood's chosen few}), \mention{some}, \mention{we}, or \mention{us}: the determiners a plural noun takes. Nearly all the lines without one of these have \mention{a}, taken up below.\footnote{COCA, searched by hand for \mention{lucky}, \mention{chosen}, \mention{fortunate}, \mention{remaining}, and \mention{priveledged} (misspelled in the search) before \mention{few}: of 321 lines with no following noun, 192 have \mention{the}, 90 \mention{a} alone, 25 a demonstrative, 6 a genitive, 2 \mention{some}, 2 \mention{we} or \mention{us}, one a name, and three nothing (a headline, a fragment, and a web post). Web, Indian, and Nigerian sources have more bare cases (\mention{work with \bk{select few} lenses}; \mention{the group of \bk{vicious \fu{few}} who \ldots}, legit.ng), with ordinary adjectives as well as \mention{select}, so the gap may not hold beyond standard English. What differs there may be the article rather than \mention{few}, which I haven't tested; on both analyses these NPs lack a determiner.}

Adjectives heading a nominal vary in the same way, word by word. The \mention{rich} type takes \mention{the} or a genitive (\mention{the world's poor}, \mention{our elderly}), though \textit{CGEL} says these NPs are \enquote{determined by the definite article} (p.~417).\footnote{In 500 COCA lines of \texttt{[apps*] poor|rich|elderly}, 19 have the adjective heading the NP after a genitive pronoun (\mention{care for \bk{their \fu{elderly}}}, \textit{New York Times}).} Some of its words take other determiners as well. \mention{Unemployed}, which \textit{CGEL} lists in the type, takes \mention{many} and \mention{some} in edited news (\mention{\bk{many \fu{unemployed}} say they are having trouble getting hired}, \textit{Washington Post}, 2021, NOW), while \mention{these rich} and \mention{some educated} turn up mostly in blogs and in news outside Britain and the United States.\footnote{NOW has 34 lines of \mention{unemployed} heading the nominal after \mention{many} or \mention{some} (\texttt{many/some unemployed [v*]}); \texttt{these rich [v*]} gave 14 across COCA, iWeb, and NOW, and \mention{some educated} three in NOW. \textcite[7]{glass2019} gives further web examples (\mention{Some fired say \ldots}, \mention{Many educated are far from role models}).} \mention{Blue} can head a nominal with no determiner (\mention{I like blue}), and \mention{second} one after \mention{a} (pp.~416--417). Both analyses state the range word by word.

What ordinary Head gives is the same function for \mention{few} in each of these uses: \mention{few would dare}, \mention{few of them}, \mention{the few}, and \mention{the lucky few}. In each, \mention{few} heads the nominal and has no other function; the determiner, if there is one, is another word, as \mention{the} is in \mention{the few}. Fusion gives the first two Det--Head and the last two Mod--Head, so the same word heading the same kind of NP gets a different pair of functions depending on whether a determiner precedes it.

Which adjectives can head a nominal is stated the same way on both analyses. \textit{CGEL} gives types, such as superlatives (\mention{the tallest}), ordinals (\mention{the second}), colours (\mention{I like \bk{\fu{blue}}}), and the special interpretation of \mention{the rich} (pp.~416--417), and excludes \ungram{\mention{the advancing}} (p.~415), \ungram{\mention{a bigger}}, and \ungram{\mention{the main}} (p.~416). \mention{Main} is attributive-only (p.~558), so modifier is its only function, and its exclusion is \textit{CGEL}'s evidence that \enquote{the possibility of fusion with the head must in the last resort be stated in syntactic terms} (p.~416). Ordinary Head states the same, as permissions to head a nominal.


The genitive gives no evidence for the modifier function. \textit{CGEL}'s argument from the genitive concerns the determiner function (p.~420), and it states no case requirement for modifiers, so the check here is this paper's own: whether the genitive divides the fused modifier--heads, some taking it and others not, as it divides the determiner--heads (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}). It does. \mention{The latter's} and \mention{the former's} are well attested, \mention{the poor's} occurs in edited prose (\mention{\bk{the \fu{poor}'s} material well-being}, \textit{Newsweek}), \mention{the rich's} only on the web, and \mention{few} after a determiner almost never takes it.\footnote{In one capped COCA display of \mention{the latter}, \mention{the former}, \mention{the other}, \mention{the poor}, and \mention{the rich} followed by \mention{'s}, \mention{the poor} has 8 genitives (five in magazines and academic writing) and \mention{the rich} 3 (all web or blog). \texttt{the few/many 's} gave none, and \texttt{few 's} one after a determiner (\mention{\bk{the first \fu{few}'s} salty freshness}, \textit{Ploughshares}). A cardinal after a determiner takes it freely: \mention{the two's} in 21 of 34 lines, ten in edited prose.} Here too, as with the determiner--heads, the functions these heads share don't predict which take the genitive.

Mod--Head is also where the advantage Section~\ref{sec:det-head} conceded to fusion has to be weighed. A bare determinative takes no pre-head adjective outside a few exclamations, taken up below (\ungram{\mention{Lucky few escaped}}),\footnote{\mention{Precious few would dare} isn't an exception. For \mention{precious} in \mention{They had precious little money}, \textcite[102]{matthews2014} offers the predicative use as the crucial evidence that it's an adverb (\mention{The money they had left was precious little}), and the same test works for \mention{few} (\mention{Volunteers were precious few}). So \mention{precious} is a modifier in the DP, as \mention{very} is in \mention{very few}, not in the nominal.} and fusion derives the gap from adjacency: with \mention{few} as Det--Head, \mention{lucky} would be in the nominal and yet before the determiner. After \mention{the}, \mention{few} is Mod--Head and \mention{lucky} modifies the nominal it heads. Ordinary Head states a condition: a nominal headed by a determinative takes a pre-head modifier only when the NP has a determiner (\mention{hardly} in \mention{hardly anyone} modifies the determinative, inside its DP).

\mention{A lucky few} looks like an exception to both fusion's derivation and ordinary Head's condition. Of the 321 COCA lines with an adjective before \mention{few} and no noun after it, 90 have \mention{a} as the only word before the adjective, and where a verb agrees with the phrase, it's plural: \mention{\bk{\fu{a lucky few}} were sent far and wide \ldots} (\textit{Washington Post}). The adjective can have its own modifier (\mention{A very lucky few}, CNN). \textit{CGEL} has two analyses that could apply. In one, \mention{a few} is a complex determinative whose internal structure is \enquote{sui generis}, admitting \mention{very}, \mention{good}, and \mention{fair}, and it's put among the lexical determinatives \enquote{as with the cardinal numerals} (p.~392). In the other, \mention{a} begins a sequence such as \mention{a further} or \mention{a good}, which allows a plural head only if a numeral or \mention{few} quantifies the head, as in \mention{\bk{A further few volunteers} were needed} (p.~353), and in \mention{a marvellous hundred days} \enquote{\mention{a} is determiner and quite independent of \mention{hundred}} (p.~385 n.~26).

Neither of \textit{CGEL}'s treatments of \mention{a few} favours fusion or ordinary Head. If \mention{a} is the NP's determiner, \mention{a lucky few} is built like \mention{the lucky few}: \mention{lucky} modifies the nominal after the determiner, so adjacency is undisturbed and ordinary Head's condition is met. If \mention{a} belongs to the determinative, so does \mention{lucky}: it modifies \mention{few} inside the DP, not the nominal. Either treatment needs a statement, a list of the sequences with \mention{a} that take a plural head or a slot after \mention{a} inside the determinative, and fusion and ordinary Head need the same one. That's all the comparison needs: on either treatment, \mention{a lucky few} leaves the gap before a bare determinative standing.

For PHP (p.~584), a condition like ordinary Head's is \enquote{precisely the kind of stipulation which is not required when the determiner and head functions are fused}. They say it of a silent-head analysis of the compounds, but it applies to ordinary Head's condition too. Whether the grammar needs such a statement anyway turns on the other heads with no determiner in their primary use, pronouns and proper nouns, and on three questions: whether such a head takes a pre-head dependent at all, whether it takes a non-restrictive adjective, and whether a restrictive one needs a determiner.

On the first, the grammar already makes a statement of this kind. Pronouns, which by definition \enquote{do not permit determiners} (p.~429), \enquote{do not normally allow internal pre-head dependents} (\ungram{\mention{Extravagant he bought a new car}}, p.~430), though they take some after the head (\mention{you who worked on both the projects}, p.~429). The compounds pattern the same way, with dependents after the head but not before, and for them \textit{CGEL} derives the gap from fusion (p.~423); a pronoun has no determiner function to derive it from. Ordinary Head's condition differs from the pronouns' restriction only in mentioning a determiner, which pronouns never take. On this question, then, ordinary Head's statement looks like one the grammar already has, and fusion's derivation saves only that one.

On the second, proper nouns take a few non-restrictive adjectives with no determiner (\mention{poor Henry}; p.~520), and the core personal pronouns take \mention{lucky}, \mention{poor}, and \mention{silly} (\mention{Lucky you!}; p.~430). In this exclamation a singular count noun doesn't need its determiner either (\mention{Poor man!}, \mention{Lucky girl!}), and some fused determiner--heads take the adjective too: the compounds (\mention{Poor everybody!}, \mention{Poor somebody!}) and \mention{all} and \mention{both} in partitives. Other partitives are untested. Five are attested, four just after the same exclamation with a proper noun or a pronoun: \mention{Poor Val. Poor everybody.} (\textit{New Yorker}), \mention{Lucky you. Lucky everyone.} (\textit{Everwood}),\footnote{COCA, \texttt{poor|lucky|silly everybody|everyone|somebody|someone}: these 2 of 28 lines; in the rest the adjective is predicative, comes before a clause (\mention{You're lucky someone called it in}), or follows a determiner, and no line has \mention{somebody} or \mention{someone} in the use.} \mention{Lucky you. \ldots{} lucky both of us actually.} (\textit{Interior Collective}), \mention{\enquote{Poor Ev,} the father concludes. \ldots{} Poor all of us.} (\textit{OC Weekly}), and, answering a mention of \mention{somebody's character}, \mention{\enquote{Poor somebody!}} (F.~Anstey, \textit{The Giant's Robe}, 1884). Simple determinatives standing alone don't take it, definite or not, even there (\ungram{\mention{Lucky few!}}, \ungram{\mention{Poor many!}}, \ungram{\mention{Lucky you. Lucky both!}}, \ungram{\mention{Poor Ev. Poor all!}}), so the gap doesn't come from an indefinite referent.\footnote{This assumes the exclamation has no silent determiner, which would put \mention{few} after a determiner, where both analyses allow the adjective (\mention{the lucky few}). \textit{CGEL} gives the comparable NPs none: pronouns \enquote{do not permit determiners} (p.~429), \mention{lucky} in \mention{Lucky you!} is a pre-head dependent (p.~430), and vocatives take a bare singular count noun with an adjective (\mention{old chap}, \mention{young man}; pp.~522--523).}

For \textit{CGEL} the compounds are fused determiner--heads, for which a pre-head dependent is \enquote{not possible} (p.~423), and so are \mention{all} and \mention{both} in partitives (pp.~376, 412), so adjacency excludes the adjective there too. Fusion could make the compounds nouns by conversion, like \mention{a nobody} (p.~423 n.~43), though that won't extend to \mention{all of us}, or list both as exceptions to adjacency. If it takes the adjective as a modifier of the whole NP, though \textit{CGEL} treats \mention{lucky} in \mention{Lucky you!} as a pre-head dependent (p.~430), ordinary Head can do the same, and the exclamation says nothing about adjacency. Otherwise ordinary Head adds the compounds and these partitives to \textit{CGEL}'s exception for \enquote{a few adjectives such as \mention{lucky}, \mention{poor}, \mention{silly} with the core personal pronouns}, and keeps simple determinatives standing alone out of it. Each analysis needs a further statement, and as analysed here the exclamation gives neither a clear advantage, though the evidence is five attestations and my judgments.

The third question is where pronouns and proper nouns look most like determinatives, and the likeness comes from definiteness. A restrictive adjective after a determiner is routine with personal pronouns (COCA has 350 tokens of \mention{the real me}). \textit{CGEL} would presumably call the word a common noun here, as it calls \mention{he} and \mention{she} in \mention{Is it a he or a she?} (p.~429 n.~46), though the reflexive suggests a secondary use of the pronoun (\mention{The 12-year-old me is kicking myself}). Countability isn't why it needs a determiner, since plural \mention{us} needs one too (\mention{the real us}, \ungram{\mention{Real us never showed up}}). For proper nouns \textit{CGEL} gives the reason: in their primary use names are \enquote{inherently definite} and so don't select determiners as ordinary heads do, while in secondary uses that definiteness is lost and determiners are \enquote{selected in the ordinary way} (p.~520), as in \mention{the young Isaac Newton}, with \mention{young} restrictive (p.~521). A pronoun restricted to one manifestation of its referent needs a determiner in the same way.

A plural common noun needs no determiner, since its bare form has a reading of its own, and a name's secondary use can be such a noun (\mention{Lucky survivors were airlifted out}, \mention{Young Kennedys still go into politics}). \mention{Few} can't: its use for a group, as in \mention{the few} and \mention{a few}, never occurs bare (\mention{Few were the hangers-on} can't mean that the small group were). \mention{Lucky} modifies that use, so it needs a determiner (\ungram{\mention{Remaining few were the hangers-on}}, beside \mention{The remaining few were the hangers-on}, \textit{Christian Century}). Fusion's adjacency predicts the gap only if bare \mention{few} is a determiner--head, not a modifier--head (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}), and the same fact rules out the modifier--head. Given it, fusion gets the gap from structure and ordinary Head from what the adjective modifies. The compounds go the same way: with an adjective they're nouns for \textit{CGEL} (p.~423 n.~43), count with a determiner (\mention{a new everything}, \textit{Rolling Stone}) or non-count without one (\mention{instant everything}, \textit{New York Times}), while as determinatives they take none (\ungram{\mention{nice anyone}}).

So ordinary Head's condition is of a kind the grammar already has, with a source in what the adjective modifies, and fusion's derivation of the pre-head gap, the one fact of form that favours the determiner function (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}), saves less than PHP suggest.

Nothing in the form of Mod--Head expressions, then, shows the modifier function.
