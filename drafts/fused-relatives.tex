\section{Fused relatives: where the second function shows}\label{sec:fused-relatives}

Sections~\ref{sec:det-head} and~\ref{sec:mod-head} found one fact of form that favours a second function in Det--Head expressions, the missing adjective, and none in Mod--Head ones. Fused relatives are the remaining test. \textit{CGEL} brings fusion to them in so many words: \enquote{we invoke the same concept of functional fusion in our analysis of fused relatives} (p.~1073).\footnote{Transparent free relatives, as in \mention{He made \bk{what may appear to be a radically new proposal}} \citep[248]{grosu-2003-unified-theory-tfrs}, raise different questions and are set aside here.}

\ea\label{ex:fr}
\ea \mention{I spent \bk{\fu{whatever} he gave me}.} (\textit{CGEL}, p.~1068)
\ex \mention{\bk{\fu{Whoever} told you that} can't have read the report.} (p.~1076)
\ex \mention{I'll go \bk{\fu{wherever} you go}.} (p.~1068)
\z\z

In (\ref{ex:fr}a), the NP \mention{whatever} heads the nominal within the object NP and is also prenucleus (the fronted phrase's function; Section~\ref{sec:analyses}) of the relative clause, with a gap after \mention{gave me} (\textit{CGEL}, p.~1073; Figure~\ref{fig:fusedrel}a). In (\ref{ex:fr}b), \mention{whoever} has the same head function in the subject NP and is also subject of the relative clause, which PHP (p.~579 n.~16) call \enquote{fused head-subject}. The criterion asks whether the function inside the relative clause shows in form.

Ordinary Head is available here too, with \mention{whomever I see} an NP headed by \mention{whomever} and a bare relative as modifier in its nominal, just as \mention{person} heads \mention{the person I see}. It's the analysis \textcite{bresnan1978free} proposed: the relativized phrase heads the whole NP, and \enquote{there is no fronted element in the relative clause} (PHP, p.~578). Bresnan and Grimshaw also require the head to agree with the gap, a condition taken up below with case. At first sight, the analysis needs nothing beyond ordinary Head, a kind of relative clause the grammar already has, and that condition, and I'll call it the head analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:fusedrel}b).

The evidence that fused relatives are NPs holds on either analysis: agreement, subject--auxiliary inversion, no extraposition, a preposition left stranded (\mention{what she referred to}, not \ungram{\mention{to what she referred}}), and the functions of NPs (\textit{CGEL}, pp.~1068--1070). So does what the literature calls matching, in which the relativized phrase's category suits \enquote{categorial selection both in the matrix clause and in the relative clause} \citep[349]{vanriemsdijk-2006-free-relatives}. \textcite[336--338]{bresnan1978free} derive the match with the matrix from headedness, since a phrase has the category of its head, and on either analysis, the match with the gap comes from the relative clause. So category matching doesn't separate the two analyses, and nor do the \mention{-ever} forms PHP cite as a formal mark of fused relatives (p.~576), which go with the construction both analyses recognize. Three other properties do, the third less directly.

First, in standard English bare relatives can't relativize their subject. \textit{CGEL} contrasts \mention{\bk{The car I took} was Ed's} with \ungram{\mention{\bk{The car hit us} was Ed's}}, and the restriction holds whatever the head: \nonstd{\mention{\bk{Anyone wants this} can have it}} is non-standard (p.~1055).\footnote{\textit{CGEL} gives two less certain cases, in an \mention{it}-cleft and an existential (\marg{\mention{It was my father \bk{did most of the talking}}}, \marg{\mention{There's someone at the door \bk{wants to talk to you}}}), which fall \enquote{at the boundary between very informal and non-standard} (p.~1055). It ties them to position: there the relative clause \enquote{could not be misconstrued as predicate of the matrix clause}. A fused relative as subject is where that misconstrual could arise (\mention{\bk{\fu{Whoever} told you that} can't have read the report}), so these cases give the head analysis no parallel.} \mention{Anyone} is a compound determinative and the ultimate head of its NP on either analysis (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}). So that example has the head analysis's structure, a head with a bare relative, and it fails. Holding the frame constant gives the same contrast with a fused relative:

\ea\label{ex:subj}
\ea \mention{I will do business with \bk{\fu{whichever of them} comes up with the cash}.} (PHP, p.~579 n.~16)
\ex \nonstd{\mention{I will do business with \bk{any of them comes up with the cash}.}}
\ex \mention{I will do business with \bk{\fu{whichever of them} you recommend}.}
\ex \mention{I will do business with \bk{any of them you recommend}.}
\z\z

With an object gap, \mention{any of them} takes a bare relative freely (\ref{ex:subj}d), so what rules out (\ref{ex:subj}b) is subject relativization. On the fused analysis, \mention{whichever of them} is itself the subject of the relative clause.\footnote{On \textit{CGEL}'s analysis, \mention{whichever} is fused twice here. Inside its own NP, it's a fused determiner--head (\textit{CGEL}, p.~398), and that NP is fused head and subject in the relative construction, which PHP count as \enquote{multiple instances of fused functions} (p.~579 n.~16). \mention{Whichever} alone, in \mention{I'll take \bk{\fu{whichever} he gave me}}, is fused twice in the same way, with head and prenucleus. Ordinary Head removes the inner fusion, for which the only evidence of form is the missing pre-head adjective (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}), and keeps the outer one, which this section's first two properties favour, since \mention{whichever} has no case forms to show the third (Section~\ref{sec:costs}; Figure~\ref{fig:fusedrelthis}).} The clause has no subject gap, and the restriction doesn't apply. The head analysis has to give relative words a permission that the restriction withholds from every other head.

The second function needn't belong to the relative word alone. The contrast is the same with a noun, as in \mention{cutting the sponge up into \bk{\fu{whatever size} fits}} (\textit{Coral Sea Dreaming: Awaken}) against \nonstd{\mention{cutting the sponge up into \bk{any size fits}}}. There \mention{whatever} is only the determiner, so the second function belongs to the NP \mention{whatever size}, whose ultimate head is the noun, as PHP (p.~572 n.~8) say of \mention{whatever meals}.

Second, other heads take infinitival relatives without a relative word, as in \mention{the ideal person \pn{for you} to confide in} (\textit{CGEL}, p.~1067), but a fused relative can't be infinitival: \ungram{\mention{I can't afford \bk{\fu{what} to buy}}} (p.~1073). \textcite[364]{kim-2017-free-relatives} also gives \ungram{\mention{He is still wearing \bk{\fu{what} to wear to bed}}}.

Without a further restriction, the head analysis allows \mention{what to buy}, as it allows \mention{a thing to buy}. On the fused analysis, \mention{what} would be the relative phrase of an infinitival relative clause, and there \enquote{the relative phrase must consist of preposition + NP} (p.~1067), as in \mention{the ideal person \bk{in whom} to confide}. The fused element of an NP fused relative is an NP, so it never qualifies.\footnote{\textit{CGEL} finds \enquote{no evident explanation} for that restriction (p.~1067), and the argument takes it as stated. \textit{CGEL} also says infinitival relatives with a relative phrase occur only as integrated relatives (p.~1067), which would exclude fused ones directly. The restriction on the relative phrase covers NP fused relatives without that statement. It doesn't cover a PP fused relative whose relative phrase is a preposition with an NP, as in \mention{I'll work \bk{\fu{in whichever city} they want me to work}} (PHP, p.~575). That \ungram{\mention{I'll work \bk{\fu{in whichever city} to work}}} is out too rests on the statement about integrated relatives.}

Third, \textit{CGEL} names case as the form in which a second function would show (pp.~420, 1073--1074), and for fused relatives with \mention{who} and \mention{whoever}, it states the requirement: the pronoun \enquote{has to satisfy the case requirements of both the relative clause and the matrix clause} (p.~1073).

\ea\label{ex:case}
\ea \mention{\bk{\fu{Whoever} is responsible for the damage} must pay for it.} (\textit{CGEL}, pp.~1073--1074)
\ex \mention{He will criticise \bk{\fu{whomever} she brings home}.}
\ex \marg{\mention{\bk{\fu{Whomever} he marries} will have to be very tolerant.}}
\ex \marg{\mention{She lunches with \bk{\fu{whomever} is going her way after morning classes}.}}
\z\z

In (\ref{ex:case}c), the relative clause calls for accusative and the matrix for nominative. In (\ref{ex:case}d), it's the reverse. \textit{CGEL} judges both \enquote{at best very questionable} (p.~1074).

On the head analysis, \mention{whomever} stands outside the relative clause, so unless case is among the features that agree with the gap, its form answers to the matrix alone. That was the analysis's attraction for matching: the head \enquote{would automatically bear the case of the whole NP} (PHP, p.~578). \textcite{bresnan1978free}'s agreement condition between the head and the gap is where case could come in, and they report that languages differ on whether case is among the features that must agree (pp.~353, 373--374). With case included, the head analysis can make \mention{whomever} answer to the clause too, but only by a further statement, where fusion gets the same result from the relative word's function inside the clause. Without case in the condition, the head analysis predicts (\ref{ex:case}c) to be degraded but not (\ref{ex:case}d). There the fused relative is the object of \mention{with}, where accusative is the formal choice (\textit{CGEL}, pp.~465--466). That (\ref{ex:case}d) is degraded too reflects the subject function inside the relative clause.

Usage shows the same thing, as a preference. The figures show which form speakers and writers choose, not which is acceptable, so this evidence is graded where the first two properties are categorical. After a preposition, the matrix function, the function of the whole fused relative, is the same in nearly every token, so the head analysis without the agreement condition gives no reason for the choice of form to differ with what follows. In COCA, \mention{whoever} and \mention{whomever} occur after a preposition 802 times before a pronoun subject, mostly relativizing a non-subject, as in \mention{with whomever they \ldots}, and among the lines that do, \mention{whomever} has about 52\%. They occur there 2,458 times before a verb, relativizing the subject, as in \mention{to whomever is \ldots}, and \mention{whomever} has 352 of those (14\%).\footnote{COCA, \texttt{[i*] who*ever [p*]} and \texttt{[i*] who*ever [v*]}. Tokens before \mention{it} are left out, since \mention{it} can be the subject of a clause that relativizes a predicative complement (\mention{whoever it is}). Of all 802 tokens before a pronoun, \mention{whomever} has 395 (49\%). A check of about 200 lines of each form found non-subject relativization in all but one \mention{whomever} line and in 131 of the 148 \mention{whoever} lines; without the others, the share is about 52\% (about 393 of 753). Further checks are in the evidence index.} Both counts follow a preposition, so a preceding preposition can't by itself explain the difference between about 52\% and 14\%, which goes with the function relativized inside the clause.

So the function inside the relative clause shows in the form. The matrix function shows too, and not only after prepositions (Table~\ref{tab:case}). In a fused relative that's the main clause's subject, \mention{whomever} is rare even when a non-subject is relativized (\mention{\bk{\fu{Whomever} I chose for this experiment} should be close to the mean}, \textit{Southern Review}).\footnote{Left out: exhaustive conditionals (\mention{Whoever you are, \ldots}), interrogative for \textit{CGEL} (p.~1072 n.~15), and relativized predicative complements (\mention{find whoever it is}); content-clause subjects are counted apart. The samples and queries are in the evidence index. The 8-of-51 cell is small, so its share is rough.}

In both columns the share falls from preposition to verb to subject, following the function of the whole phrase, and in each row, it's several times higher when a non-subject is relativized, following the function inside the clause. Prepositions and verbs both call for accusative, so the step between those rows isn't a difference in the case the matrix function calls for; in writing alone, the non-subject column narrows to 65\% against 58\%, so part of it is register. Where the cells can be split, the order holds in speech and scripts and in writing taken separately, though \mention{whomever} is far rarer in the first.\footnote{Where the fused relative is the subject of a verb's content clause (\mention{I think \bk{\fu{whoever} did this to her} wants \ldots}, \textit{Wildling}), \mention{whomever} has 9 of about 400 tokens with subject relativization, about 2\%, as for the main clause's subject. The cells split into spoken, TV, and film lines against the rest are in the evidence index; fiction has dialogue and web texts are informal, so the split is rough.} 

The form answers to both functions. \mention{Whoever} is the default: in the pooled figures, \mention{whomever} is commonest where both functions call for accusative (about 52\% and 37\%), and it's a minority in the clashes, one of which is (\ref{ex:case}c). Fusion with \textit{CGEL}'s case requirement predicts that a clash has no fully acceptable form, which is what \textit{CGEL}'s question marks record; \textit{CGEL} adds that \mention{whoever} \enquote{would be preferable in both, but many would regard it as less than fully acceptable in formal style} (p.~1074). Usage shows both functions at work, though how strongly each counts, and how the two interact, I leave open. So case favours fusion here rather than forcing it.

\textit{CGEL} drew this analogy itself. Its explanation of the missing genitive in \ungram{\mention{many's expenses}} is that the form's \enquote{case should match both functions}, and it points to \enquote{the discussion of case in fused relatives} (p.~420). Among fused determiner--heads, the genitive divides the words: my searches found very few tokens of \mention{many's}, but \mention{either's}, \mention{each's}, and \mention{everyone's} occur. The functions, which all these words share on \textit{CGEL}'s analysis (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}), don't predict that division, and \mention{the poor's} puts the genitive on a fused modifier--head (Section~\ref{sec:mod-head}). In fused relatives, the word stays the same and the form follows the function inside the relative clause, as a preference. On the diagnostic \textit{CGEL} itself chose, the two constructions come apart.

\textit{CGEL} also has PP fused relatives (\mention{I'll go \bk{\fu{wherever} you go}}, p.~1068), and PHP (p.~575) add AdjPs and AdvPs (\mention{I'll be \bk{\fu{however responsive} they want me to be}}). The case test doesn't apply to them, but the first property can: in \mention{Come round \bk{\fu{whenever} suits you}}, the head analysis would have \mention{whenever} heading the PP, with a bare relative that has a subject gap, again a head with a permission no other head has.

So the relative word's place in the clause shows in form in three ways, two of them categorical and one graded. The head analysis can state each, but only by giving fused-relative heads statements no other head needs: subject relativization without a relative word, no infinitival form, and case among the features its head shares with the gap. Fusion gets all three from the rules for relative clauses that have a relative phrase. So a second function is favoured here, though not forced. The subject and infinitival facts point to a function for the relative phrase inside the clause, but they don't choose among fusion, movement, and multidominance as ways of giving it one.

A dependency grammar draws a related line: \textcite[2]{gisborne2026mutual} disprefers mutual dependency where an analysis without it is coherent, and keeps it for free relatives, where the \mention{wh} word heads the construction and is also extracted (p.~16).
