\section{Fused relatives: where the second function shows}\label{sec:fused-relatives}

PHP (p.~571) list the dependents that fusion involves in English as \enquote{determiner, modifier and, in fused relative clauses, subject or prenucleus}. Sections~\ref{sec:det-head} and~\ref{sec:mod-head} found one fact of form that shows a second function in Det--Head expressions, the missing adjective, and none in Mod--Head ones. Fused relatives are the remaining test. \textit{CGEL} brings fusion to them in so many words: \enquote{we invoke the same concept of functional fusion in our analysis of fused relatives} (p.~1073).\footnote{Transparent free relatives, as in \mention{He made \bk{what may appear to be a radically new proposal}} \citep[248]{grosu-2003-unified-theory-tfrs}, raise different questions and are set aside here \citep{grosu-2003-unified-theory-tfrs,grosu-2016-semantics-syntax-morphology-tfrs}.}

\ea\label{ex:fr}
\ea \mention{I spent \bk{\fu{whatever} he gave me}.} (\textit{CGEL}, p.~1068)
\ex \mention{\bk{\fu{Whoever} told you that} can't have read the report.} (p.~1076)
\ex \mention{I'll go \bk{\fu{wherever} you go}.} (p.~1068)
\z\z

In (\ref{ex:fr}a), \mention{whatever} is head of the object NP and prenucleus of the relative clause, with a gap after \mention{gave me} (\textit{CGEL}, p.~1073; Figure~\ref{fig:fusedrel}a). In (\ref{ex:fr}b), \mention{whoever} is head of the subject NP and also the subject of \mention{told you that}, which PHP (p.~579 n.~16) call \enquote{fused head-subject}. The criterion asks whether the function inside the relative clause shows in form.

Ordinary Head is available here too. \mention{Whomever I see} could be an NP headed by \mention{whomever}, with a bare relative as modifier in its nominal, just as \mention{person} heads \mention{the person I see}. Put in \textit{CGEL}'s terms, it's the analysis \textcite{bresnan1978free} proposed: the relativized phrase heads the whole NP, and \enquote{there is no fronted element in the relative clause} (PHP, p.~578). At first sight it needs nothing beyond ordinary Head and a kind of relative clause the grammar already has, and I'll call it the head analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:fusedrel}b).

Much comes out the same. The evidence that fused relatives are NPs holds on either: agreement, subject--auxiliary inversion, no extraposition, a preposition left stranded (\mention{what she referred to}, not \ungram{\mention{to what she referred}}), and the functions of NPs (\textit{CGEL}, pp.~1068--1070). So does what the literature calls matching, in which the relativized phrase's category suits \enquote{categorial selection both in the matrix clause and in the relative clause} \citep[349]{vanriemsdijk-2006-free-relatives}. \textcite[336--338]{bresnan1978free} derive the match with the matrix from headedness, since a phrase has the category of its head, and on either analysis the match with the gap comes from the relative clause. So category matching doesn't separate the two analyses, and nor do the \mention{-ever} forms PHP cite as a formal mark of fused relatives (p.~576), which go with the construction both analyses recognize. Three other properties do.

First, in standard English bare relatives can't relativize their subject. \textit{CGEL} contrasts \mention{\bk{The car I took} was Ed's} with \ungram{\mention{\bk{The car hit us} was Ed's}}, and the restriction holds whatever the head: \nonstd{\mention{\bk{Anyone wants this} can have it}} is non-standard (p.~1055). \mention{Anyone} is a compound determinative and the ultimate head of its NP on either analysis (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}). So that example has the head analysis's structure, a head with a bare relative, and it fails. Holding the frame constant gives the same contrast with a fused relative:

\ea\label{ex:subj}
\ea \mention{I will do business with \bk{\fu{whichever of them} comes up with the cash}.} (PHP, p.~579 n.~16)
\ex \nonstd{\mention{I will do business with \bk{any of them comes up with the cash}.}}
\ex \mention{I will do business with \bk{\fu{whichever of them} you recommend}.}
\ex \mention{I will do business with \bk{any of them you recommend}.}
\z\z
% (b)--(d) constructed; judgments confirmed by Brett, 17 Oct 2026.

With an object gap, \mention{any of them} takes a bare relative freely (\ref{ex:subj}d), so what rules out (\ref{ex:subj}b) is subject relativization. On the fused analysis, \mention{whichever of them} is itself the subject of \mention{comes up with the cash}.\footnote{On \textit{CGEL}'s analysis, \mention{whichever} is fused twice here. Inside its own NP it's a fused determiner--head (\textit{CGEL}, p.~398), and that NP is fused head and subject in the relative construction, which PHP count as \enquote{multiple instances of fused functions} (p.~579 n.~16). \mention{Whichever} alone, in \mention{I'll take \bk{\fu{whichever} he gave me}}, is fused twice in the same way, with head and prenucleus. Ordinary Head removes the inner fusion, for which the only evidence of form is the missing pre-head adjective (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}), and keeps the outer one, which this section's three properties show (Figure~\ref{fig:fusedrelthis}).} The clause has no subject gap, and the restriction doesn't apply. The head analysis has to give relative words a permission that the restriction withholds from every other head. The contrast is the same with a noun, as in \mention{cutting the sponge up into \bk{\fu{whatever size} fits}} (\textit{Coral Sea Dreaming: Awaken}) against \nonstd{\mention{cutting the sponge up into \bk{any size fits}}}. There \mention{whatever} is only the determiner, so the second function belongs to the NP \mention{whatever size}, whose ultimate head is the noun, as PHP (p.~572 n.~8) say of \mention{whatever meals}.

Second, other heads take infinitival relatives without a relative word, as in \mention{the ideal person \pn{for you} to confide in} (\textit{CGEL}, p.~1067), but a fused relative can't be infinitival: \ungram{\mention{I can't afford \bk{\fu{what} to buy}}} (p.~1073). \textcite[364, 378]{kim-2017-free-relatives} also gives \ungram{\mention{He is still wearing \bk{\fu{what} to wear to bed}}} and \ungram{\mention{Tom will do \bk{\fu{whatever} to satisfy her}}}.

Without a further restriction, the head analysis allows \mention{what to buy}, as it allows \mention{a thing to buy}. On the fused analysis, \mention{what} would be the relative phrase of an infinitival relative clause, and there \enquote{the relative phrase must consist of preposition + NP} (p.~1067), as in \mention{the ideal person \bk{in whom} to confide}. The fused element of an NP fused relative is an NP, so it never qualifies.\footnote{\textit{CGEL} finds \enquote{no evident explanation} for that restriction (p.~1067), and the argument takes it as stated. \textit{CGEL} also says infinitival relatives with a relative phrase occur only as integrated relatives (p.~1067), which would exclude fused ones directly. The restriction on the relative phrase covers NP fused relatives without that statement. It doesn't cover a PP fused relative whose relative phrase is a preposition with an NP, as in \mention{I'll work \bk{\fu{in whichever city} they want me to work}} (PHP, p.~575). That \ungram{\mention{I'll work \bk{\fu{in whichever city} to work}}} is out too rests on the statement about integrated relatives.}

Third, case. \textit{CGEL} names case as the form in which a second function would show (pp.~420, 1073--1074), and for fused relatives with \mention{who} and \mention{whoever} it states the requirement: the pronoun \enquote{has to satisfy the case requirements of both the relative clause and the matrix clause} (p.~1073).

\ea\label{ex:case}
\ea \mention{\bk{\fu{Whoever} is responsible for the damage} must pay for it.} (\textit{CGEL}, pp.~1073--1074)
\ex \mention{He will criticise \bk{\fu{whomever} she brings home}.}
\ex \marg{\mention{\bk{\fu{Whomever} he marries} will have to be very tolerant.}}
\ex \marg{\mention{She lunches with \bk{\fu{whomever} is going her way after morning classes}.}}
\z\z

In (\ref{ex:case}c), the relative clause calls for accusative and the matrix for nominative. In (\ref{ex:case}d) it's the reverse. \textit{CGEL} judges both \enquote{at best very questionable} (p.~1074).

On the head analysis as stated so far, \mention{whomever} stands outside the relative clause, so its form should answer to the matrix alone. That was the analysis's attraction for matching: the head \enquote{would automatically bear the case of the whole NP} (PHP, p.~578). \textcite{bresnan1978free} add an agreement condition between the head and the gap, and languages differ on whether case is among the features that must agree: the head takes the subordinate verb's case in Finnish but not in Old English (pp.~353, 373--374). With case included, the head analysis can make \mention{whomever} answer to the clause too, but only by a further statement, where fusion gets the same result from the relative word's function inside the clause. Without it, the head analysis predicts (\ref{ex:case}c) to be degraded but not (\ref{ex:case}d). There \mention{whomever} is the object of \mention{with}, where accusative is the formal choice (\textit{CGEL}, pp.~465--466). The question mark on (\ref{ex:case}d) shows the subject function inside the relative clause.

\textit{CGEL}'s two dialects for \mention{whom}, if they carry over to \mention{whomever}, point the same way. When \mention{who} is the subject of a clause embedded in a relative, as in \dialect{\mention{those \bk{whom} he thought were guilty}}, Dialect~A takes its case from that function, and Dialect~B from its \enquote{prenuclear position preceding the subject of the relative clause} (\textit{CGEL}, p.~467). Both rules look inside the relative clause, and the head analysis puts \mention{whomever} outside it, so it needs a condition linking the head's form to the relative clause here too. Agreement with the gap gives Dialect~A's rule but not Dialect~B's, which goes by position.

Usage shows the same thing, as a preference. After a preposition the matrix function, the function of the whole fused relative, is the same in nearly every token, so the head analysis without the agreement condition gives no reason for the choice of form to differ with what follows. In COCA, \mention{whoever} and \mention{whomever} occur after a preposition 802 times before a pronoun subject, as in \mention{with whomever they \ldots}, and \mention{whomever} has 395 of those (49\%, or about 52\% once the gaps are checked). They occur there 2,458 times before a verb, where the gap is the subject, as in \mention{to whomever is \ldots}, and \mention{whomever} has 352 of those (14\%).\footnote{COCA, \texttt{[i*] who*ever [p*]} and \texttt{[i*] who*ever [v*]}. Tokens before \mention{it} are left out, because \mention{it} can be the subject of a clause whose gap is a predicative complement, as in \mention{whoever it is}; of the 180 such tokens, \mention{whomever} has 35. The verb list shows only its 1,000 most frequent types, so its counts are lower bounds; in its 319 most frequent rows, 43 \mention{whoever} tokens follow a preposition that can introduce a clause (\mention{like}, \mention{after}, \mention{until}), too few to move the share by half a point. A check of about 200 lines of each form found a non-subject gap in all but one \mention{whomever} line before a pronoun other than \mention{it}, and in 131 of the 148 such \mention{whoever} lines. The other \mention{whoever} lines have a predicative gap (\mention{for whoever she is}), the subject of an embedded clause (\mention{whoever you think is guilty}), or a fused relative that is the subject of a clause after \mention{until} or \mention{before} (\mention{until whoever you're waiting for gets here}). Without them the share is about 52\% (about 393 of 753).}

So the function inside the relative clause shows in the form. The matrix function shows too, and not only after prepositions (Table~\ref{tab:case}). As the subject of the main clause, \mention{whomever} is rare even with a non-subject gap (\mention{\bk{\fu{Whomever} I chose for this experiment} should be close to the mean}, \textit{Southern Review}).\footnote{In COCA, for the main clause's subject, all the \mention{whomever} hits after a full stop and 500-line samples of the \mention{whoever} hits, leaving out exhaustive conditionals (\mention{Whoever you are, \ldots}), interrogative for \textit{CGEL} (p.~1072 n.~15); for a verb's object, about 200 lines of each form after a lexical verb, leaving out predicative gaps (\mention{find whoever it is}) and counting content-clause subjects apart. The queries and counts are in the evidence index. The 8-of-51 cell is small, so its share is rough.}

In both columns the share falls from preposition to verb to subject, and in each row it's several times higher with a non-subject gap. Prepositions and verbs both call for accusative, so the step between those rows isn't a difference in the case the matrix function calls for; in writing alone the non-subject-gap column narrows to 65\% against 58\%, so part of it is register. Where the fused relative is the subject of a verb's content clause (\mention{I think \bk{\fu{whoever} did this to her} wants \ldots}, \textit{Wildling}), \mention{whomever} has 9 of about 400 tokens with a subject gap, about 2\%, as for the main clause's subject. Where the cells can be split, the order holds in speech and scripts and in writing taken separately, though \mention{whomever} is far rarer in the first.\footnote{Taking spoken, TV, and film lines as one group and the rest as the other, \mention{whomever} has about 27\% and 65\% of the preposition-object cell with a non-subject gap, 11\% and 58\% of the verb-object one, 1 line of 21 and 29\% of the main clause's subject with a non-subject gap, 2\% and 9\% of the verb-object cell with a subject gap, and under 1\% and 3\% of the main clause's subject with a subject gap. The preposition-object cell with a subject gap comes from frequency lists and can't be split. Fiction has dialogue and web texts are informal, so the split is rough.} 

So the form answers to both functions. \mention{Whoever} is the default: in the pooled figures, \mention{whomever} comes near half only where both functions call for accusative, and it's a minority in the clashes, one of which is (\ref{ex:case}c). Fusion with \textit{CGEL}'s case requirement predicts that a clash has no fully acceptable form, which is what \textit{CGEL}'s question marks record; \textit{CGEL} adds that \mention{whoever} \enquote{would be preferable in both, but many would regard it as less than fully acceptable in formal style} (p.~1074). Usage shows both functions at work, though how strongly each counts, and how the two interact, I leave open. So case favours fusion here rather than forcing it.\footnote{\textcite[357]{vanriemsdijk-2006-free-relatives} finds that case matching concerns \enquote{the actual morpho-phonological shape of the case form}: in German, where nominative and accusative \mention{was} have the same form, a mismatch of the abstract cases passes. Which function's case wins a clash varies across languages (PHP, p.~578).}

\textit{CGEL} drew this analogy itself. Its explanation of the missing genitive in \ungram{\mention{many's expenses}} is that the form's \enquote{case should match both functions}, and it points to \enquote{the discussion of case in fused relatives} (p.~420). Among fused determiner--heads, the genitive divides the words: \mention{many's} is all but absent, but \mention{either's}, \mention{each's}, and \mention{everyone's} occur. The functions, which all these words share on \textit{CGEL}'s analysis (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}), don't predict that division, and \mention{the poor's} puts the genitive on a fused modifier--head (Section~\ref{sec:mod-head}). In fused relatives the word stays the same and the form follows the function inside the relative clause, as a preference. On the diagnostic \textit{CGEL} itself chose, the two constructions come apart.

\textit{CGEL} also has PP fused relatives (\mention{I'll go \bk{\fu{wherever} you go}}, p.~1068), and PHP (p.~575) add AdjPs and AdvPs (\mention{I'll be \bk{\fu{however responsive} they want me to be}}). The case test doesn't apply to them, but the first property can: in \mention{Come round \bk{\fu{whenever} suits you}}, the head analysis would have a bare relative with a subject gap. Both analyses can treat the four types as one construction, so the types don't separate them, but on the head analysis it's again a construction whose head has permissions no other head has, since \enquote{standard relative clauses are modifiers only within Nom} (p.~575).

So the second function shows in form in three ways, two of them categorical and one graded. The head analysis can state each, but only by giving fused-relative heads statements no other head needs: subject relativization without a relative word, no infinitival form, and a condition linking the head's case to a relative clause it isn't part of. Fusion gets all three from the rules for relative clauses that have a relative phrase.\footnote{A constructional analysis reaches the same double relation by another route. \textcite[376]{kim-2017-free-relatives} makes the free relative construction a subtype of both the head-filler and the head-modifier constructions, so the relativized phrase is at once head and filler.} So fusion is favoured here, though not forced. Fused relatives are the only construction in which this paper keeps it (Figure~\ref{fig:fusedrelthis}).

A dependency grammar draws a related line: \textcite[2]{gisborne2026mutual} disprefers mutual dependency where an analysis without it is coherent, and keeps it for free relatives, where the \mention{wh} word heads the construction and is also extracted (p.~16).
