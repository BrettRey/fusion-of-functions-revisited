Above the line, the content is shared: the lists, defaults, and conditions have to be stated on both analyses, as Sections~\ref{sec:det-head}--\ref{sec:once} found, and each phrases them in its own terms: fusion as licences for a fused function, ordinary Head as permissions to head a nominal.

Below the line is the trade, and fusion has a good case. The grammar keeps constituents with two functions for fused relatives anyway (Section~\ref{sec:fused-relatives}), so giving one to a determinative that stands alone adds no new kind of structure, and it derives a restriction ordinary Head has to state: no pre-head modifier in a nominal headed by a bare determinative. The added function matches the word's dependent use (\mention{few} is determiner in \mention{few students} and modifier in \mention{the few students}), which keeps fusion's licences general, but the match is how the function is chosen, not evidence for it. Ordinary Head states the condition instead, a statement of the kind \textit{CGEL} already makes for pronouns (p.~430; Section~\ref{sec:mod-head}), and gains parsimony: \mention{few} has one function in all its uses as head, and constituents with two parents stay in one construction. The cost it avoids is one of extent, not of kind. The compounds' restrictor, which PHP also credit to fusion, adds nothing to either side (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}).

The two departures PHP (pp.~570--571) distinguish fall differently too. Fusion puts both on one constituent wherever a DP, AdjP, or PP is a fused head, since that phrase has two functions and is also where the category changes. Ordinary Head never puts both on one constituent. Its constituents with two functions are in fused relatives, where the fused phrase and its parent must project the same lexical category (p.~574), so no category changes there. In a fused relative with \mention{whichever} alone, such as \mention{I'll take \bk{\fu{whichever} he gave me}}, the two departures fall in one NP at different nodes: the DP heads the nominal, and the NP is head and prenucleus at once (Section~\ref{sec:fused-relatives}, Figure~\ref{fig:fusedrelthis}).

On the criterion of Section~\ref{sec:analyses}, shape, other dependents, the determiners taken, and the genitive give no evidence for either function (Sections~\ref{sec:det-head} and~\ref{sec:mod-head}). For the determiner function the comparison available is between a determinative standing alone and the same word after a determiner, since \textit{CGEL} gives determinatives no use as a pure head, and it changes two things at once: the word loses its determiner function, and a determiner precedes it. A fact that separates the two uses could follow from either change, so it counts for the determiner function, but for less than a contrast in the function alone would.

The pre-head adjective does show something, and it counts for fusion, given the Det--Head choice (Section~\ref{sec:det-head}). Against that fact are the fewer functions ordinary Head assigns and the constituent with two parents that fusion gives every NP headed by a bare determinative. The comparison is close, and the burden of proof, which Section~\ref{sec:analyses} puts on the second function, decides it: I take the balance to favour ordinary Head, as a reasonable preference, not a forced conclusion. The balance would shift if adjacency predicted further restrictions on bare determinatives, beyond pre-head modifiers in the nominal, that ordinary Head could only list.

The saving is in where fusion applies, not in whether the grammar has it: ordinary Head keeps constituents with two functions for fused relatives and removes them from noun phrases without a relative clause, giving each word fewer functions in its uses as head.
