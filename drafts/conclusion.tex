\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

On the commitments held fixed in Section~\ref{sec:analyses}, this paper finds no property of form that shows the modifier function where \textit{CGEL} fuses head and modifier in a nominal (\mention{the lucky few}, \mention{the rich}; Sections~\ref{sec:analyses} and~\ref{sec:mod-head}), the clearest of its results. PHP's \mention{once}, \mention{twice}, and \mention{thrice} add nothing further (Section~\ref{sec:once}). For the determiner function (\mention{take some}), it finds one, the absence of an adjective before a bare determinative, which fusion derives from adjacency and ordinary Head states, largely as the grammar states the pronouns' restriction (Sections~\ref{sec:det-head} and~\ref{sec:mod-head}). An exclamation that lets a pronoun take the adjective lets the compounds and partitive \mention{all} and \mention{both} take it too, though not a simple determinative standing alone, so it gives neither analysis a clear advantage (Section~\ref{sec:mod-head}). Against the gap, ordinary Head assigns fewer functions and keeps constituents with two parents to one construction; the comparison is close, and the balance favours ordinary Head without forcing it (Section~\ref{sec:costs}).

Noun-phrase fused relatives supply the evidence the criterion asks for, in two properties categorically and in a third as a preference (Section~\ref{sec:fused-relatives}). The relative phrase's function inside its clause shows in subject relativization and in the ban on infinitival fused relatives, and in usage, the case of \mention{whoever} answers both to that function and to the function of the whole phrase. So the evidence favours a second function there, though it doesn't force one, and \textit{CGEL} represents the two functions as fused. The grammar defended here keeps fusion where form earns it, in fused relatives, not wherever a noun phrase lacks a noun.
