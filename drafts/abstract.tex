\begin{abstract}
When does one constituent have two functions at once? \textit{The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language} treats \mention{some} in \mention{take some} as both determiner and head of its noun phrase, and \mention{few} in \mention{the lucky few} as both modifier and head. \textcite{Payne2007} make this fusion of functions explicit and build their case on \mention{once}, \mention{twice}, and \mention{thrice}. The alternative treats the word as a head and nothing more. To choose, this paper proposes a test: a property of form counts as evidence for a second function if it varies with that function rather than with the word. Using the grammar's own diagnostics and corpus data, I find none for the modifier function, nothing further in Payne et al.'s constructions, and one for the determiner function: the absence of an adjective before a bare determinative (\ungram{\mention{Lucky few escaped}}). That fact favours fusion, but on balance treating the word as a head is preferable: it assigns fewer functions and confines constituents with two parents to fused relatives. Fused relatives differ. Subject relativization and the ban on infinitival forms reflect the relative word's function inside its clause, and in usage the case of \mention{whoever} answers to that function and to the function of the whole phrase. Fusion is favoured there, though not forced.
\end{abstract}
