\section{Determinatives standing alone: Det--Head}\label{sec:det-head}

A determinative can make up a whole noun phrase, or head one that also contains a complement or modifier, and so can an independent genitive such as \mention{mine} (e):

\ea\label{ex:dh}
\ea \mention{I'll take \bk{\fu{some}}.}
\ex \mention{\bk{\fu{Few}} would dare underestimate Aaron Finch's men.}
\ex \mention{\fu{some} of the wine}, \mention{\fu{many} of them}, \mention{\fu{whichever} of them}
\ex \mention{hardly \fu{anyone} present}, \mention{\fu{something} reliable}
\ex \mention{\bk{\fu{Mine}} are ready.}
\z\z

For \textit{CGEL}, each of these has a fused Det--Head: one phrase functions at once as determiner of the NP and as head of its nominal (pp.~410--423). On ordinary Head, it's a pure head, with no second function (Section~\ref{sec:analyses}). The question here is whether anything in the form of these expressions shows the determiner function.

Both analyses take these to be complete NPs with no silent noun. They have the functions of NPs (\mention{Some left}, \mention{I see some}, \mention{with some}), there's no noun to restore (\ungram{\mention{many ones of us}}; pp.~420--421), and often there's none in the context either.\footnote{Of 94 NOW lines for \mention{few would}, 82 have no noun phrase or named set in the displayed context that could supply the set, as in (\ref{ex:dh}b) (\textit{Daily Times}, Pakistan).} Nor does definiteness separate them: an NP of bare \mention{this} is definite and one of bare \mention{some} isn't, as before a noun, so the word fixes the NP's definiteness on either analysis.

Where the context names no set, as in (\ref{ex:dh}b), a domain the context supplies restricts it, as it restricts any quantified expression, even one with a noun; for \mention{few}, \mention{many}, \mention{all}, and \mention{some} a convention also gives \enquote*{people} (Section~\ref{sec:analyses}). \Textcite[43]{vonfintel1994} represents the domain as an index on the quantifying word, not a constituent, so it isn't the silent noun set aside in Section~\ref{sec:analyses}, and it says nothing about which determinatives can stand alone; both analyses list them (Table~\ref{tab:costs}, Section~\ref{sec:costs}).

A determinative with dependents is analysed as a bare one is. In \mention{some of the wine}, the \mention{of} phrase is a complement inside the nominal (\textit{CGEL}, pp.~411--412), so \mention{some} is the ultimate head on either analysis, as \mention{whichever} is in \mention{whichever of them} (Section~\ref{sec:fused-relatives}). So it is with relative clauses,\footnote{\textcite[184--185]{wu2021exocentric} finds a dilemma for fusion in \mention{For \bk{\fu{some} who made the journey into Wales}, \ldots} (BNC): fusion makes \mention{some}, a determiner, the antecedent of the relative clause, against the principle that determiners don't count as antecedents, and he adds an empty noun to resolve it. On ordinary Head, \mention{some} isn't a determiner here: it heads the nominal that the relative clause modifies, with nothing added.} and with complements licensed by a noun in the antecedent, as in \mention{We have a proposal to close the petition and \bk{\fu{another} to keep it open}} (Scottish Parliament, Public Petitions Committee, \textit{Official Report}, 16 December 2008).\footnote{The infinitival is a complement of the kind \mention{proposal} licenses (\textit{CGEL}, p.~1259), so on either analysis, its licence, like its reading, comes from the antecedent. \textit{CGEL} has a PP complement in the same position in \mention{Helen's of the Rachmaninov} (p.~421). The antecedent licenses the complement but not the head: \ungram{\mention{Their names weren't on the list of the dead, nor on the of the missing}} is out, with \mention{the} read as \enquote*{the list}, because \mention{the} doesn't stand alone (p.~1508).}

\textit{CGEL} and PHP point to three places where the determiner function might show: the correspondence with the dependent use; the morphological forms of \mention{none}, \mention{mine}, and the compounds; and case. The function shows in none of them, though one fact of order, taken up below, favours it.

First, the correspondence. \textit{CGEL} keeps the determiner function because the word \enquote{can occur in the dependent position in NPs where it is not fused with the head} (p.~410). It keeps the category because the word takes the same modifiers in both uses (\mention{not much cheese}, \mention{not much of the cheese}; pp.~420--421). Ordinary Head keeps the category, and with it both facts: \mention{much} is the same determinative in both uses, and it takes the dependents a determinative takes.

What the correspondence adds beyond the category is a list of the determinatives that can stand alone. \mention{Some}, \mention{each}, and \mention{either} can, and \mention{every} normally can't, though NOW has two published lines with it standing alone, whose status I leave open (\mention{Every has a story}, \textit{Patch}; \mention{Every has different blocking mechanics}, \textit{Guardian}); \mention{no} does so only in the form \mention{none}. PHP (p.~581) state the list as a \enquote{lexical distinction between determinatives that allow [fusion of functions] and those that do not}. Ordinary Head states the same list as a permission to head a nominal with no determiner. Neither analysis gets it free.

Second, form. PHP find formal differences between fused and non-fused functions here, as in fused relatives: \mention{no} \enquote{has a specialized morphological variant \mention{none} which is restricted to the fused function} (p.~581). A difference of form doesn't say which function it marks, though, and the genitive pronouns supply the test. They alternate the same way (\mention{my}, \mention{mine}), and \textit{CGEL} states the conditioning for both pairs: \enquote{the dependent forms are used in (pure) determiner function, the independent ones in head function} (p.~410).

\mention{Mine} occurs with a determiner function and without one. The independent genitive pronouns \enquote{occur in fused heads and also pure heads} (p.~410), pure heads being heads with no second function, and predicative \mention{mine} in \mention{Everything that is \bk{mine} is yours} has \enquote{no basis for a fused-head analysis} (p.~469). Its form is the same, so the form doesn't mark the determiner function. \mention{None} has no pure-head use to test (p.~410), so its form is assigned to the same conditioning on the model of \mention{mine}, not shown independently. Ordinary Head states both alternations as \textit{CGEL} does, by function: Det against Head.

Compounds raise the question of form again. \textit{CGEL} analyses \mention{anyone}, \mention{something}, and the rest as fused determiner--heads (p.~423). PHP take them to be determinatives because \enquote{they take exactly the same pre-head modifiers as the determinative bases they contain} (p.~581): \mention{not everything} beside \mention{not every house}, \mention{hardly anyone present} beside \mention{hardly any writer present}. That fixes the category, which ordinary Head keeps.

On ordinary Head, \mention{not} and \mention{hardly} are modifiers in the DP that heads the nominal, as they are in the DP functioning as determiner in \mention{hardly any writer present} (\textit{CGEL}, p.~431), so the compound's base decides which ones it takes. PHP also take the compounds' morphology, a determinative base with a nominal base, to mirror fusion (pp.~565, 587--588). The two bases can as well reflect the compound's meaning, a quantifier with a sort (\enquote*{person}, \enquote*{thing}), and both analyses give it that meaning. Simple \mention{some} and \mention{few} have no nominal base, and for PHP, they're fused just the same.

The compounds' modifiers divide by side: adverbs before, adjectives after (\mention{hardly anyone present}, \mention{something reliable}). The adjective is a \term{restrictor}, a single post-head dependent that can be a simple adjective, of the kind that would normally precede a noun (p.~423; PHP, p.~583). PHP count fusion's account of the division \enquote{a particular strength} (p.~583). On ordinary Head, the compound's DP heads the nominal and the restrictor follows it in the nominal, so the two analyses group the phrase alike, as \mention{\bk{\bk{hardly anyone} present}}.

Nor does fusion account for the restrictor's position by itself. \textit{CGEL} puts the adjective after the head \enquote{because determiner and head are fused}, which rules out internal pre-head dependents (p.~423). But the restrictor stays where nothing is fused. \textit{CGEL} treats \mention{something} in \mention{a little something} as a noun formed by conversion (p.~423 n.~43), and a single adjective follows it (\mention{a little something extra}), as one does in \mention{a pre-emptive something fabulous} (CBS). With a common noun as head, a postpositive adjective occurs only \enquote{under restricted conditions} (p.~529).\footnote{In COCA, 43 of 46 relevant lines of an article, an adjective, \mention{somebody} or \mention{something}, and a second adjective are \mention{a little something} (\mention{extra} in most). The evidence beyond the formula is thin, but NOW, searched for \mention{someone}, has \mention{a certain someone special} twice (\textit{Independent Online}, 2013; hindustantimes.com, 2025).} So fusion isn't necessary for the position.

Fusion doesn't determine which words take a restrictor either. Simple determinatives standing alone are fused too, and \mention{all}, \mention{much}, and \mention{little}, which share \mention{else} with the compounds (p.~423), take a restrictor (\mention{little new}). But after \mention{few}, \mention{some}, or \mention{none}, a single adjective follows only if it's comparative or one, like \mention{present}, that follows nouns too (\mention{I've heard worse but none weirder}, film script; \mention{None present objected}; \ungram{\mention{There are few good}}). A defender of fusion can let the converted noun inherit the restrictor and list the words that take it, and then the post-head side gives fusion no advantage beyond the pre-head one.

The pre-head side does favour fusion, and it's the only fact in the distribution of these words that does. Bare determinatives take no pre-head adjective (\ungram{\mention{nice anyone}}, \ungram{\mention{Lucky few escaped}}), apart from a few exclamations in which the compounds and partitive \mention{all} and \mention{both} take one (\mention{Poor everybody!}, \mention{Poor all of us!}; Section~\ref{sec:mod-head}). Nouns converted from the compounds, such as \textit{CGEL}'s \mention{a little something} (p.~423 n.~43), fall outside the restriction (Section~\ref{sec:mod-head}). With \mention{few} as both determiner and head, an adjective modifying the nominal would come before the NP's determiner, where nothing in the nominal can go, so fusion derives the gap from PHP's condition that the fused functions are adjacent (p.~571); ordinary Head has to state it (Section~\ref{sec:mod-head}), as an analysis with a silent head does (PHP, p.~584).

The derivation needs the bare word to be a fused determiner--head, not a modifier--head like \textit{CGEL}'s \mention{blue} in \mention{I like blue} (p.~417), and for \mention{many}, as in \mention{many of us}, \textit{CGEL} gives no argument that picks determiner over modifier. The gap favours determiner over modifier: a bare modifier--head would leave room for an adjective before it in the nominal, as \mention{the lucky few} has after its determiner, and only the determiner--head leaves none (\ungram{\mention{Poor many!}}; Section~\ref{sec:mod-head}). That's one fact favouring one analysis over two rivals, not a second fact for fusion.

The adjective is fine once a determiner precedes (\mention{the lucky few}). It's also fine in \mention{a lucky few}, which \textit{CGEL}'s analyses of \mention{a few} allow two ways (pp.~353, 392). Section~\ref{sec:mod-head} shows that on either of \textit{CGEL}'s treatments of \mention{a few}, \mention{lucky} doesn't modify a nominal headed by a bare determinative, so the gap stands.

Third, case, where \textit{CGEL} says the determiner function would show. In \mention{many people's expenses}, the genitive \mention{'s} attaches to \mention{people}, the head of \mention{many people's}, and not to its determiner, \mention{many}. On the fused analysis, a bare \mention{many} is both head and determiner, so \ungram{\mention{many's expenses}} would put the genitive on \mention{many}, which never carries it as a determiner, though a genitive can itself be the determiner (\mention{my}, \mention{Kim's}). \textit{CGEL} reports that very gap, calling \mention{all's}, \mention{many's}, \mention{few's}, and \mention{some's} \enquote{completely excluded}. Its explanation is fusion: \enquote{the form is simultaneously determiner and head, and for full acceptability its case should match both functions} (\textit{CGEL}, p.~420).

\textit{CGEL} offers this against analysing these words as pronouns, an analysis that gives no reason for the genitive gap (p.~420). Ordinary Head gives no reason either, so the argument bears on it too, and it's the most direct of the three.

For \mention{all}, \mention{many}, \mention{few}, and \mention{some}, my searches found the gap nearly complete,\footnote{COCA searches for each word followed by \mention{'s} find genitives on \mention{either}, \mention{neither}, and \mention{each} standing alone in 35 lines, 13 of them in edited prose, against four for \mention{many} and \mention{some}, all on the web (two seem fine to me: \mention{to \bk{\fu{many}'s} suprise \textup{[sic]}}, \mention{out of \bk{\fu{so many}'s} reach}), none for \mention{few}, and none for \mention{all} in a capped display; the counts aren't adjusted for how often each word stands alone. \mention{Another} followed by \mention{'s} and a noun, not preceded by \mention{one}, has 1,331 tokens, 798 in edited prose; \textit{CGEL} mentions the genitive only for reciprocal \mention{one another's} (pp.~428, 1502).} but it doesn't hold across the fused determiner--heads. \mention{Either's}, \mention{neither's}, \mention{each's}, and \mention{another's} occur in edited prose, and I find them acceptable: \mention{increase \bk{\fu{either}'s} share of its market} (\textit{TIME}), \mention{\bk{\fu{Neither}'s} numbers are all that encouraging} (\textit{Washington Post}), \mention{the highlight of \bk{\fu{each}'s} day} (\textit{Atlantic}). \textit{CGEL} calls \mention{each's} \enquote{occasionally attested but of somewhat questionable status} (p.~420). COCA's twelve genitives of \mention{each} bear out \enquote{occasionally attested}, but five are in edited prose, so they aren't confined to informal writing.

\textit{CGEL}'s examples of genitive marking on the head include \mention{everyone's} and \mention{somebody's} (p.~479), compounds it analyses as fused determiner--heads (p.~423). A defender of fusion could put their genitive down to the nominal base, \mention{-one} or \mention{-body}.

So the genitive divides these words, and the argument from case faces a dilemma. If \mention{many's} is out because its case would have to match two functions (p.~420), \mention{either's}, \mention{neither's}, \mention{each's}, and \mention{another's} should be out too, since \textit{CGEL} gives those words the same two functions. If something else is needed, the gap gives fusion no demonstrated advantage. Either way, the shared functions don't predict where the division falls, though function may still play a part. As stated, neither analysis predicts it: fusion has to exempt \mention{either}, \mention{neither}, \mention{each}, \mention{another}, and the compounds unless their nominal base carries the genitive, and ordinary Head has to list the words that don't take it, as it lists the reflexive pronouns, which have no genitive either (p.~426).

Among determinatives standing alone, the division may follow number: those that take the genitive, the compounds included, take singular agreement, and those that don't (\mention{all}, \mention{many}, \mention{few}, \mention{some}) take plural agreement or have a non-count reading. Number can't decide after a determiner, where plural heads differ (\mention{the poor's} and \mention{the two's}, but hardly ever \mention{the few's}; Section~\ref{sec:mod-head}), but if it decides for bare determinatives, neither analysis needs a list. A comparison that could show a function would keep the word and change only that function, and these words offer none, since \textit{CGEL} gives them no use as the pure head of a nominal.

Section~\ref{sec:fused-relatives} finds that kind of evidence in fused relatives, where the form of \mention{whomever} follows the function inside the relative clause.

Apart from the pre-head adjective, both analyses need further statements of the same facts, and nothing else in the form of these expressions shows a determiner function.\footnote{Licensing \mention{ever} doesn't show it either. \textit{CGEL} separates determiner from modifier \mention{few} by clause negativity (p.~394), but the adjective \mention{scant}, a modifier for \textit{CGEL} (p.~556), licenses \mention{ever} in its clause when no determiner precedes it, as determiner \mention{few} does (\mention{scant regard was ever given to the people who lived there}, \textit{Northern Daily Leader}). Whether clauses with bare \mention{scant} pass \textit{CGEL}'s other tests for negative clauses is untested.} Sections~\ref{sec:mod-head} and~\ref{sec:costs} weigh the pre-head adjective against what ordinary Head gains.
