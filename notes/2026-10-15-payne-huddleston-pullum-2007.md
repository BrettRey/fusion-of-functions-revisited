## Their position, fairly stated

- **Aim.** *Once*, *twice* and *thrice* are "primarily compound determinatives, analogous in many respects to expressions like *someone* and *somewhere*" (p. 565), so *I only met her once* has an NP *once*, not an adverb. The analysis "provides further evidence for the existence of fusion of functions as a distinct syntactic configuration, and indirectly supports theoretical frameworks which treat functions and categories as distinct primitives" (p. 565).
- **Framework.** CGEL's function/category distinction; Det is a function, determinative a category. They reject the DP hypothesis (n. 2, p. 566) and use "DP" for determinative phrases, as this paper does.
- **Fusion defined (p. 568–571).** One constituent realizes a head function and a dependent function (simple case), or a head and a dependent of a dependent (complex case, fused relatives). Six properties (repeated as (39), p. 598):
  - (i) one category realizes two functions;
  - (ii) only head + immediate dependent, or head + dependent of an immediate dependent;
  - (iii) the fused functions are adjacent;
  - (iv) the fused category is typically not the category of XP;
  - (v) XP projects the category of the ultimate head in any counterpart construction;
  - (vi) the fused category is the dependent's category in any counterpart construction.
- **Two departures from canonical structure.** The two-mother configuration: "Although each fused function in principle has its own mother …, the single category which realizes them will in effect have two mothers. This is the major departure from canonical principles of structure which FF entails" (p. 570), citing Sampson's single mother condition. And the category change: "The second departure is of course the possibility noted above that a phrasal constituent of category XP may have a category other than X as its ultimate head. As already noted, this second departure will be independently required in the case of hybrid constructions" (p. 571). Also: the fused node is "a point in the normal path of headedness at which there can be a change of category (a property of FF which is shared with hybrid constructions …)" (p. 569).
- **Counterparts aren't required.** FF constructions are "constructions in their own right, licensed by independent rules"; they and their counterparts "frequently differ in other respects … and in some cases no counterpart even exists" (p. 570).
- **Determinative licensing is lexical.** "*every* cannot occur as fused determiner-head (*\*every of the students*), and … *no* has a specialized morphological variant *none* which is restricted to the fused function. The transparent relation between form and function in our approach, together with the independently motivated lexical distinction between determinatives that allow FF and those that do not, provides for a very natural description of such facts" (p. 581).
- **Compounds (§1.3, pp. 581–584).** *Someone*, *anyone* etc. are fused Det–Head because "they take exactly the same pre-head modifiers as the determinative bases they contain" (p. 581). The restrictor function (post-head, non-recursive, admits simple adjectives). "It is a particular strength of the FF analysis … that it provides an explanation for the janus-like behaviour of forms such as *anyone* which have adverbs as premodifiers but adjectives and adjective phrases as postmodifiers" (p. 583). Against Larson & Marušič's null-headed analogue, which needs "a stipulation that pre-head AdjPs are not permitted when the head is null"; under FF "the impossibility of an intervening pre-head AP follows automatically from the adjacency requirement …, and this itself ultimately follows from the more fundamental constraint against crossing branches" (p. 584).
- ***Once* (§2).** Five uses (numerical, singulative, past, term-of-office, sequential). Numerical *once* takes *the* and *this* (*only the once*, *this last once*), *every* (*every once in a while*), relatives (*the once that we're doing now*), pre-head modifiers of *one* (*at least once*, *more than once*), and *more* (*once more*). Without a determiner it's fused Det–Head; with one it's fused Mod–Head, licensed by *one*'s modifier use (*this one time*), (19), p. 588. *\*last once* is excluded "for the same reason as *\*last one time*", by adjacency (p. 589). Sequential *once* is a preposition (p. 594).
- ***Twice*, *thrice* (§3).** The same, plus multiplier *twice* as predeterminer (*twice the amount*). Non-standard *the twice that of the fundamental* is Mod–Head (p. 597).
- **FF against its neighbours (§5).** Incorporation shares only property (v). Hybridization (*there's [no stopping him]*, NP with a VP head) shares the category change, "However, there is no fusion of functions in this case" (p. 600). Hybrids are "found only marginally in English" (p. 566).

## What each claimed advantage is argued against

| Advantage | Rival named | Bears on ordinary Head? |
|---|---|---|
| Adjacency automatic (fused relatives, *\*the Ø present whoever …*, *\*(this) once last Ø*) | null heads (p. 571 n. 5, p. 576, p. 589) | No: ordinary Head has no null head |
| Formal differences between fused and non-fused forms (*whatever*/*which*; *none*/*no*) | null heads (p. 576) | Partly: ordinary Head states *no*/*none* as form selection by dependent vs independent use |
| Case matching in fused relatives | Bresnan & Grimshaw; Groos & van Riemsdijk (pp. 576–579) | No: fused relatives are outside this paper; fusion is kept there |
| No pre-head adjective with Det–Head compounds | Larson & Marušič (p. 584) | **Yes.** Ordinary Head has to state it (see below) |
| *Once* with and without a determiner | dictionaries' noun analysis (p. 587) | Ordinary Head handles it as with cardinals (*the two*, *these three*) |

Nowhere do they consider an analysis in which the determinative is an ordinary Head of Nom. The closest is hybridization, which they set aside as marginal in English.

## Consequences for this paper

1. **The cleanest statement of the thesis is theirs.** Fusion makes two departures: a category change on the head path (shared with hybrids, "independently required", p. 571) and a constituent with two parents ("the major departure", p. 570). Ordinary Head keeps the first and drops the second. Both analyses give independent *some* the ultimate head D (their property (iv)); they differ only in whether the DP also fills Det. Their remark that hybrids are marginal in English (p. 566) doesn't count against ordinary Head specifically, since fusion has the same category change.
2. ***The some who* still discriminates, as a dilemma.** (Revised after Brett: "the 'in some cases no counterpart even exists' claim covers their asses but it's not a great theory".) CGEL's licence is stated "in general" (p. 410) and by enumeration ("The cases where fusion is possible are as follows", (a) determinatives with an internal-modifier use, p. 415); *the some who* falls outside it. The escape is PH&P's no-counterpart clause (p. 570), but their only concrete no-counterpart case is the PP, AdjP and AdvP fused relative (p. 575). Every determinative they analyse has a counterpart: *once*/*one time*, *this once*/*this one time* (p. 588), *at least once*, *more than once*. Their "lexical distinction between determinatives that allow FF and those that do not" (p. 581) restricts fusion where a counterpart exists (*\*every of the students*, *none*); it doesn't license fusion where none does. So the fusion theorist has two options: keep the counterpart condition, which is what gives fusion its predictions for determinatives, and list *the some who* as an exception; or extend the no-counterpart clause to determinatives, which turns the licence into a lexical list of which determinatives take *the*, the permissions ordinary Head already states, and leaves the shared node predicting nothing. An earlier draft of this note said the lexical licence made the two analyses tie; that conceded too much.
3. **Concede the compounds point, then bound it.** PH&P derive the absence of pre-head adjectives with bare compounds (*\*nice anyone*, *\*last once*) from adjacency. Ordinary Head states it: a determinative-headed Nom admits a pre-head adjective only under external determination, the same condition that gives *the lucky few* and *this last once*. One condition either way, but theirs follows from a general principle and this one doesn't. The premodifier half of their "particular strength" is neutral (CGEL p. 437; test 2 note in the determinatives repo).
4. ***Once*, *twice*, *thrice* fit in a subsection.** Under ordinary Head, numerical *once* is a compound determinative heading NP, externally determinable as cardinals are (*the once that we're doing now* like *the two that I've seen*), with *at least*, *more than*, *more* as for its base. *Every once in a while* has *once*'s base *one* with a modifier use (*every one time* is the counterpart they'd cite), so it fits the counterpart licence; it isn't a parallel to *the some who* (checked, 15 Oct, at the advisor's suggestion). Nothing in §§2–4 needs more than the compounds section's conditions.
5. **Ellipsis section.** Their pp. 583–584 comparison with Larson & Marušič (2004) and Leu (2004), and n. 5 (p. 571) on null heads and adjacency, are the starting points. Their adjacency argument applies to ellipsis analyses of *the some who* as well.
