**Abstract.** The abstract changed in one phrase ("answers to both its functions"). Paste it as plain text:

> Some grammars give a word two syntactic functions at once. The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language treats some in take some as both the determiner and the head of its noun phrase, and rich in the rich as both modifier and head, a fusion of functions that Payne et al. (2007) defend. The simpler alternative is that the word is just the head. I argue that a second function needs evidence of form that varies with it rather than with the word, and test for such evidence with the grammar's own diagnostics and new corpus data. For the modifier function, there's none. For the determiner function, there's one fact, that no adjective comes before a bare determinative outside a few exclamations, but the comparison is close, and on balance, it doesn't justify the extra function. Fused relatives such as whoever told you that are different: they relativize subjects, they have no infinitival forms, and in usage, the case of whoever answers to both its functions. There a second function is favoured, though not forced. Keeping a category, heading a phrase, and taking a reading from context are things a head with no second function can do, not evidence of one.
