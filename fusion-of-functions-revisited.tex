\begin{abstract}
\textit{The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language} (\textit{CGEL}) analyses independent determinatives, as in \mention{take some} and \mention{the lucky few}, with fused functions: one word realizes Head together with Determiner or Modifier. Holding \textit{CGEL}'s separate determinative category fixed, I argue that ordinary Head relations do better. Both analyses make the determinative the ultimate head; they differ in the relations in between. On the ordinary analysis, the determinative heads a nominal that heads the noun phrase, so \mention{the lucky few} has the structure of \mention{the lucky survivors}. The discriminating case is \mention{the some who say it}. Since \mention{the} fills Det, a fused analysis must make \mention{some} a modifier-head, and for determinatives \textit{CGEL} ties that to an internal-modifier use, as \mention{the few mistakes} licenses \mention{the few}; \mention{some} has no such use, and corpus checks find \mention{the some people} only as typos.

Converting \mention{some} to a noun or adding a construction-specific rule would each add a stipulation that ordinary Head avoids, and dropping the condition would reduce fusion's licence to a lexical list, the permissions ordinary Head uses anyway. Ordinary Head also lets the complex determinatives \mention{a few} and \mention{many a} be analysed as peripheral \mention{a} and predeterminer \mention{many}, as expansion (\mention{a good few}) and agreement (\mention{a few are}, \mention{many a man is}) suggest. Fusion remains where it does independent work, with adjectives (\mention{the idle rich}).
\end{abstract}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Independent determinatives don't need fused functions. With \textit{CGEL}'s categories unchanged, an ordinary-Head analysis gives bare, partitive, and externally determined uses a single chain: the determinative heads a nominal, and the nominal heads the noun phrase. That groups \mention{the lucky few} with \mention{the lucky survivors}, not with \mention{the idle rich}, and it treats independent \mention{few} alike with and without \mention{the}. It also needs fewer constituents with two parents: fusion's shared node stays where adjectives need it, not where determinatives do.

The case that separates the analyses is \mention{the some who}. Since \mention{the} fills Det, a fused analysis has to make \mention{some} a modifier-head, and for a determinative \textit{CGEL} ties that to a use as internal modifier, which \mention{some} lacks. So fusion has two options: list \mention{the some} as an exception, as \textit{CGEL} already does for \mention{the both of us} \citep[377]{huddleston2002}, or give up the condition. \textcite[570]{Payne2007} allow fusion without a counterpart, but their one such case is the fused relative headed by a PP, AdjP, or AdvP \citep[575]{Payne2007}; every determinative they analyse has one, as \mention{this once} has \mention{this one time} \citep[588]{Payne2007}. Extending the exemption to determinatives would reduce the licence to a list of which determinatives take \mention{the}, the permissions ordinary Head already states, and leave the shared node predicting nothing.

A conversion analysis has a problem of its own. The one attested conversion of \mention{some}, a writer's \mention{nones and somes} for the religiously unaffiliated and affiliated, behaves as a converted noun does: it takes plural inflection and adjectives (\mention{Catholic or Protestant somes}) and names a group by a property. \mention{The some who} keeps the quantity reading of independent \mention{some} instead. That doesn't exclude every conversion, since nouns such as \mention{lot} quantify too, but the converted noun would have to keep exactly the properties of independent \mention{some}. Under ordinary Head no fused-head licence is needed, only the lexical permissions every determinative has, and the rarity of \mention{the some} has a plausible semantic source: existential \mention{some} adds little once \mention{the} has identified the set.

The same structure retires the complex determinatives. In \mention{a few}, \mention{a little}, and \mention{a great many}, a peripheral \mention{a} modifies an NP headed by \mention{few}, \mention{little}, or \mention{many}. \mention{Many a} has predeterminer \mention{many} before an \mention{a}-NP, like \mention{such a} and \mention{what a}. The decomposition rests on \textit{CGEL}'s own record: \mention{a few} admits \mention{very}, \mention{good}, and \mention{fair}, and agreement follows the parts, plural after \mention{a few} and singular after \mention{many a man}. Their restrictions, including the polarity contrast between \mention{few} and \mention{a few}, become conditions on those modifiers.

Fusion remains for adjectives, where it has independent work, and for the nominal \mention{nylon} and \mention{double} constructions. The open question is phrase structure. If determinative-headed and genitive expressions turn out to need different constraints once their lexical restrictions are held fixed, separate phrase types would be favoured. Nothing here depends on whether determinatives are a kind of noun. A grammar that keeps them a separate category adopts the analysis by licensing ordinary Head for both categories.
